Locating Places on the Earth

Maps, latitude-longitude and time zones — finding the 'address' of any place on Earth.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

How will you find your way in a new city?

Imagine you get off at the station of a new city for the first time and need to go to a bank. You can ask someone — or you can look at a map! A map is like a treasure map: it tells you where things are and how to reach them. In a map we see the land as if looking from above (like a bird). A book of many maps is called an atlas.

Three kinds of maps

Physical map — things of nature: mountains, rivers, seas. Political map — boundaries made by people: countries, states, capitals, cities. Thematic map — made for one particular kind of information, such as where how much rain falls, or which crop grows where.

3 important parts of a map — distance, direction, symbols

1. Scale (distance) — how does a big city fit on a small sheet of paper? With a scale! If the scale is 1 cm = 500 m, then 2 cm on the map is actually 1,000 m (1 km). 2. Direction — North is at the top, then going clockwise East, South, West — these are the 4 cardinal directions. The 4 in between are NE, SE, SW, NW. On most maps there is just one arrow marked 'N' that shows North. 3. Symbols — it is not possible to draw a picture of every building, so we use small signs: school, post office, railway line, river. For India, these symbols have been fixed by the Survey of India (a government organisation).

The Earth is round — so why a globe?

The Earth is roughly a ball (sphere), slightly flattened at the poles. When a round surface is shown on flat paper, some distortion creeps in, so a globe (a round map) is more accurate. But a globe does not fit in your pocket, so we also use flat maps.

Latitude — the 'horizontal' lines drawn on the Earth

Imagine an imaginary line around the middle of the Earth — the Equator, 0°. It is the biggest circle and divides the Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. Above and below it are more circles — the parallels of latitude — which get smaller towards the poles. The North Pole is 90°N and the South Pole is 90°S. Latitude tells us how far a place is from the Equator. It also helps us understand climate: hot (torrid) near the Equator, moderate (temperate) in the middle, and very cold (frigid) near the poles.

Longitude — the lines drawn from top to bottom

Now imagine half circles running from the North Pole to the South Pole — these are called meridians of longitude; they are all equal in length. They are counted from the Prime Meridian (0°), which passes through Greenwich in London (decided in 1884). From 0° we go to 180°E and 180°W. Latitude and longitude together make an address — for example, Delhi ≈ 29°N, 77°E. An interesting fact: India once had its own prime meridian — the 'Madhya Rekha', passing through Ujjayini (Ujjain), where the astronomer Varahamihira worked about 1,500 years ago. India stretches between 8°N–37°N and 68°E–97°E.

How does time change? — 1° = 4 minutes

The Earth makes one turn (360°) in 24 hours. So 360 ÷ 24 = 15° every hour, that is 1° = 4 minutes. The Sun rises first in the east, so places in the East are ahead in time and places in the West are behind. If every city kept its own time, there would be confusion — so a country keeps one Standard Time. IST = GMT + 5 hours 30 minutes. Big countries have many time zones — 6 in the USA, 11 in Russia!

International Date Line — yesterday or the day after in one step!

Around 180° there is a zig-zag line — the International Date Line. When you cross it, the date changes: crossing it while going east, you subtract one day; going west, you add one day. So while sitting in an aircraft, your Monday can turn into Sunday!

Oceans and Continents

Three-fourths of the Earth is water and one-fourth is land — the 5 oceans, the 7 continents and their effect on life.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Why is the Earth called the 'Blue Planet'?

From space the Earth looks blue, because about 3/4 of its surface is water and only a little more than 1/4 is land. Most of the water lies in the Southern Hemisphere, and most of the land lies in the Northern Hemisphere. In ancient India, Varuna was the god of the sea and waters — the motto of the Indian Navy is 'Sham no Varunah' (may Varuna be kind to us).

Five oceans — from biggest to smallest

Pacific (biggest) > Atlantic > Indian > Southern (Antarctic) > Arctic (smallest). In truth they are all connected — like one big ocean! The Indian Ocean has Asia to its north, Africa to its west, Australia to its east and the Southern Ocean to its south. To the west of India is the Arabian Sea and to the east the Bay of Bengal.

So much water, yet little to drink?

Sea water is salty — it is no good for drinking or farming. Fresh water is very scarce, and it is spread out in glaciers, rivers, lakes, the air (vapour) and under the ground (groundwater). That is why saving water is important.

Gifts and dangers of the sea

Our monsoon (rain) comes from the sea. But cyclones and tsunamis also come from the sea. A tsunami = a huge wave raised by an earthquake or volcano under the sea. On 26 December 2004, an earthquake in Indonesia caused a tsunami — 14 countries including India were hit, and more than 2 lakh people died; the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Tamil Nadu and Kerala were badly affected. After this the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System was set up, and in India the NDMA (National Disaster Management Authority) helps at the time of disasters.

Continents — 4 or 7!

A continent = a very large piece of land. Some people count Europe-Asia as one (Eurasia), some count both Americas as one — so the count can be anywhere from 4 to 7. The most common count is 7; by size: Asia > Africa > North America > South America > Antarctica > Europe > Australia (smallest). The 5 Olympic rings = the 5 inhabited continents: Africa, America, Asia, Australia, Europe.

Islands, and India in Antarctica

Greenland = the world's biggest island. India has more than 1,300 islands — the Andaman & Nicobar Islands (Bay of Bengal) and Lakshadweep (Arabian Sea). In ice-covered Antarctica, the Indian Antarctic Programme has been running since 1981; the first base was Dakshin Gangotri (1983), followed by 2 more bases later; about 40 teams have done research there.

Oceans — the Earth's 'lungs'

More than half of the world's oxygen is made by plants and tiny living things in the ocean — that is why the oceans are called the 'planet's lungs'. But lakhs of tonnes of plastic go into the sea every year, and overfishing is reducing marine life. 8 June = World Oceans Day (UN). We can help by using less plastic!

Landforms and Life

Mountains, plateaus and plains — each landform has its own climate, way of life, challenges and opportunities.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is a landform?

The surface of the Earth is high in some places, flat in others and deep in others. These shapes are called landforms; they take lakhs of years to form. The three main ones are: mountains, plateaus and plains. And then there are the dry deserts too.

Mountains — wide at the bottom, narrow at the top

Mountain: a wide base, steep slopes and a narrow peak. A lower one with a rounded top = a hill. Big chains: the Himalayas (Asia), the Alps (Europe), the Andes (South America). The highest peak is Mount Everest — 8,848 m (on the Nepal-Tibet border); its Tibetan name is Chomolungma ('Mother Goddess of the World'), and its Nepali name is Sagarmatha ('Goddess of the Sky'). Others: Kanchenjunga (Nepal-Sikkim), Kilimanjaro (Africa — a lone mountain, not part of any range), Mont Blanc (Alps), Aconcagua (Andes), Anamudi (Kerala — the highest in South India). The Himalayas are young mountains — sharp, high and still rising; the Aravallis are very old — worn down and rounded.

Life on mountains

At height we find conifer trees — pine, fir, spruce, deodar (their needle-like leaves let snow slide off); higher up there is only grass, moss and lichen. People live by terrace farming, herding, tourism and pilgrimage. The difficulties: landslides, avalanches (snow slides), flash floods, cloudbursts and bitter cold. The Ganga (~2,500 km) comes from the Himalayas; its tributaries include the Yamuna and Ghaghara; the Son comes from the Vindhyas. Kalidasa's Kumarasambhava begins with a tribute to the Himalayas. Mount Kailash is sacred to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and followers of Bon.

Two examples of courage

Bachendri Pal — the first Indian woman to climb Everest (1984); Padma Shri 1984, Padma Bhushan 2019. Arunima Sinha — lost a leg in an accident, yet climbed Everest in 2013, and also the highest peak of every continent; Padma Shri 2015. The lesson: with courage, no mountain is too big!

Plateaus — flat-topped 'tables'

A plateau = land higher than its surroundings, almost flat on top, with steep sides — like a big table! The Tibetan Plateau is the biggest and highest (~4,500 m) — the 'Roof of the World'. The Deccan Plateau is among the oldest plateaus in the world, formed from volcanic lava — hence its fertile black soil. Plateaus are 'storehouses of minerals': East Africa — gold, diamonds; Chhota Nagpur — iron, coal, manganese. Beautiful waterfalls: Hundru (Subarnarekha), Jog (Sharavati), Nohkalikai — 340 m (Meghalaya), and Africa's Victoria (Zambezi).

Plains — where most people live

Plains = flat land, usually less than 300 m above sea level. During floods, rivers spread the soil they carry (sediment) — very fertile! So farming is easy and the population is large: the Ganga plains have ~40 crore people (more than 1/4 of India). Crops: rice, wheat, maize, barley, millets; fibres: cotton, jute. But too much irrigation is lowering the groundwater.

Deserts, and the 5 'tinai' of the Tamil poets

Hot deserts — Sahara, Thar; cold — Gobi; some scholars call Antarctica a desert too (very little rain or snowfall). In 2,000-year-old Tamil Sangam poetry there are 5 types of land (tinai) and the life in each: Kurinji (hills — hunting), Mullai (forest/grassland — herding), Marudam (fertile plains — farming), Neydal (seashore — fishing, travel by sea), Palai (dry land — travel, war). In other words, geography shapes the way of life!

Timeline and Sources of History

How we measure time in history, the sources we learn from, and how the earliest humans lived.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

History — a conversation with the past

The historian E.H. Carr said that history is 'an unending dialogue between the present and the past'. We take today's questions and ask them of earlier times. Many experts help in this: the Geologist (soil, rocks, mountains, rivers), the Palaeontologist (fossils — traces of ancient living things preserved in rock), the Anthropologist (human society and culture), the Archaeologist (things found by digging) and the Epigraphist (who reads ancient inscriptions).

How the calendar works

Today the world uses the Gregorian calendar — 12 months, 365 days, and a leap year (366 days) every 4 years. A hidden rule: a century year is a leap year only if it is divisible by 400 — so 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not. India's Panchanga is based on the positions of the Sun and Moon — it tells us about eclipses, sunrise and sunset, and the auspicious times for festivals.

BCE and CE — backward and forward counting

Earlier people said BC/AD; now we say BCE (Before Common Era) and CE (Common Era). In BCE the counting runs backwards: 500 BCE, 400 BCE, 300 BCE… (300 BCE came after 500 BCE). In CE it runs forwards. Remember — there is no 'year zero': 1 CE comes right after 1 BCE. So how many years from a BCE date to a CE date? Add both, then subtract 1. Example: Buddha ~560 BCE → up to 2024 = 560 + 2024 − 1 = 2,583 years.

Century and millennium

A century = 100 years. The 21st century = 2001 to 2100 (yes, from 2001, not 2000!). The 3rd century BCE = 300 BCE to 201 BCE. A millennium = 1,000 years: the 3rd millennium CE = 2001–3000; the 1st millennium BCE = 1000 BCE to 1 BCE. Trick: century number = the first digits of the year + 1 (2024 → 20 + 1 = 21st).

The 'evidence' of history — sources

A historian is a detective piecing together a jigsaw puzzle — the pieces (sources) sometimes fit and sometimes clash. Sources: Literary (Vedas, Itihasas, manuscripts, poetry and plays); Archaeological (coins, inscriptions, copper plates, buildings, tools, pottery, burials); Artistic (paintings, sculptures); Oral (folk tales, songs); Foreign accounts (reports by foreign travellers); Scientific (DNA/genetics, study of ancient climate). For recent history: newspapers and TV-internet.

How did the first humans live?

Humans like us (Homo sapiens) have lived on Earth for ~3,00,000 years. In the beginning they were hunter-gatherers — living in small groups (bands), under rock shelters or in caves; they learned to make fire, stone tools, paintings on cave walls (rock paintings) and beads. When the Last Ice Age (from over 1 lakh years ago until ~12,000 years ago) ended, the climate turned warmer — people began farming and herding and settled near rivers.

From settlement to city

With farming came chieftains, and land that was shared by all (no private ownership). A small settlement (hamlet) → a village (village) → a town (town). Clay pots (pottery) appeared, and among metals first copper, later iron.

India, That Is Bharat

The ancient names of Bharat — what our own people called it, and what foreigners called it.

One country, many names — why?

At home, your mother calls you by one name and your friends by another. In the same way, our country has been called by different names by different people at different times. In this chapter we will see what our own people called the country, and what foreigners called it.

The oldest name — Sapta Sindhava

The Rig Veda, one of the oldest texts in the world, called the north-western region 'Sapta Sindhava' — the land of seven rivers. 'Sindhu' means the Indus river and also, in general, 'a river'.

Names of regions in the Mahabharata

The Mahabharata names many regions that are still recognised today: Kashmira (Kashmir), Kurukshetra (Haryana), Vanga (Bengal), Pragjyotisha (Assam), Kaccha (Kutch), Kerala. And for the whole subcontinent it uses two names: 'Bharatavarsha' and 'Jambudvipa'.

What Bharatavarsha and Jambudvipa mean

Bharatavarsha = 'the land of the Bharatas'; the name 'Bharata' first appears in the Rig Veda for a prominent Vedic group of people. Jambudvipa = 'the island of the jamun fruit'! Emperor Ashoka (~250 BCE) used 'Jambudvipa' in his inscription for the whole of India — which included parts of today's Bangladesh, Pakistan and Afghanistan. Vishnu Purana: 'the country north of the ocean and south of the Himalayas is Bharata'. A ~2,000-year-old Tamil poem: 'from Kumari in the south to the great mountain in the north, between the eastern and western seas'.

What does the Constitution say?

Our Constitution (in force since 1950) says right at the start: 'India, that is Bharat'; in Hindi, 'Bharat arthat India'. So both names are ours.

What did foreigners say?

The Persians (people of ancient Iran) conquered the Indus region in the 6th century BCE. In their language they pronounced 's' as 'h' — so 'Sindhu' → 'Hindu', 'Hind'. Here 'Hindu' was only the name of a place, not a religion! The Greeks had no 'h' sound in their language, so they dropped it too → 'Indoi' / 'Indike' → later 'India'. The Chinese said 'Yintu' / 'Yindu', and, respectfully, 'Tianzhu' ('heavenly master' — the land of the Buddha).

Xuanzang and 'Hindustan'

The Chinese monk Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang) came to India in the 7th century CE, stayed 17 years, took Buddhist texts back to China and translated them from Sanskrit into Chinese. The name 'Hindustan' first appeared about ~1,800 years ago in a Persian inscription; later, most invaders used this name. Lesson: the names kept changing, but the country's identity remained.

The Beginnings of Indian Civilisation

The Harappan / Indus-Sarasvati civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) — planned cities, water management, trade and decline.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is a civilisation?

What does a village need to become a city and then a civilisation? Think: a government/administration to run the rules; town planning — roads, water, drains; crafts; trade — within the country and beyond; writing; shared cultural ideas; and such good farming that the cities can be fed. The world's first civilisations: Mesopotamia (Iraq-Syria, ~6,000 years ago), then Egypt.

Our first urban civilisation — many names, one civilisation

The Indus-Sarasvati civilisation, c. 2600–1900 BCE. Villages grew into towns from ~3500 BCE, and by ~2600 BCE towns became cities — this is called the 'First Urbanisation of India'. Names: Indus, Harappan, Indus-Sarasvati, Sindhu-Sarasvati. 'Harappan' because Harappa (in Pakistan's Punjab) was the first to be excavated, in 1920–21. The name 'Indus Valley' is now outdated, because the civilisation spread far beyond the Indus. The Sarasvati river = today's Ghaggar (India) / Hakra (Pakistan) — now only seasonal; in the Rig Veda it is both a goddess and a river.

Which city is where?

Harappa — Punjab (Pakistan); Mohenjo-daro — Sindh (Pakistan); Dholavira — Gujarat (Rann of Kutch); Rakhigarhi — Haryana; Kalibangan — Rajasthan; Ganweriwala — Cholistan (Pakistan); Lothal — Gujarat. Along the Sarasvati in Haryana there were also Farmana, Bhirrana, Banawali.

Smart cities — 4,500 years ago!

Roads were wide and laid out by direction (N-S, E-W); a wall (fortification) ran around the city; there was an upper town (for important people) and a lower town (for ordinary people). Dholavira had 3 sections, with stone foundations. Interestingly, small and large houses had the same quality of bricks! Waste water went through covered drains under the roads. Mohenjo-daro had ~700 brick wells. Dholavira had at least 6 reservoirs — the largest 73 m long; one was cut out of rock (33 m).

The Great Bath — a puzzle

The Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro is ~12 × 7 m, waterproofed with bitumen (a tar-like substance), with a drain in one corner. It was first thought to be a public bathing tank, but since most houses had their own bathrooms, that idea was dropped. Perhaps it was a tank for a royal family or for religious rituals — scholars still debate it.

Food, farming and cotton

Barley, wheat, millets, sometimes rice, pulses, vegetables. Cotton was grown here earlier than anywhere else in the world (Eurasia)! There was a plough too — a clay model was found at Banawali. Traces of turmeric, ginger, banana and milk products have been found in pots.

Trade — across the sea

Beads of red stone carnelian from Gujarat, shell bangles; bronze (copper + tin); copper perhaps from outside. An ivory comb was found in Oman and beads in Susa (Iran). At Lothal there is a huge basin — 217 m × 36 m (a little longer than two football fields) — believed to be a dockyard (a place for ships). This was India's first major sea-trade activity.

Seals and famous objects

Seals were made of steatite (a soft stone, hardened by heating), a few cm in size; they show a unicorn, a bull, a horned tiger + signs of writing. No one has been able to read this script to this day! Seals were probably used to stamp goods in trade. Also found: a bronze mirror, stone weights, a game board, a clay whistle, the 'Priest King', the Pashupati seal, the bronze 'Dancing Girl' (10.8 cm, Mohenjo-daro), a figurine in namaste pose, and a Lothal pot with the story of the thirsty crow!

Why did the civilisation decline? (~1900 BCE)

There is no evidence of war or invasion — this was a peace-loving civilisation. Two main reasons: (1) from 2200 BCE the climate changed — less rain; (2) the Sarasvati river dried up in its middle section — Kalibangan and Banawali were abandoned. People went back to villages — but their culture (farming, art, customs) continued.

India’s Cultural Roots

The Vedas, the Upanishads, Buddhism, Jainism and tribal traditions — the roots of the tree of Indian culture.

Culture — an ancient tree

Think of our culture as a very old tree: it has many roots (different sources — some going back to Harappa), a strong trunk (shared ideas), and many branches (different traditions). In this chapter we will look at the roots and the branches.

The Vedas — a treasure of knowledge that was remembered

'Veda' comes from the Sanskrit 'vid' (to know) — it means knowledge. There are four Vedas: Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva. They contain thousands of hymns composed in Sapta Sindhava. Amazingly, they were not written down; they were memorised and recited orally for 100–200 generations almost without change! That is why UNESCO (2008) called Vedic chanting a 'masterpiece of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity'. Estimates for the date of the Rig Veda differ: from the 5th to the 2nd millennium BCE.

Rishis, rishikas and gods

The hymns were composed by rishis (men) and rishikas (women). The gods included Indra, Agni, Varuna, Mitra, Sarasvati, Ushas (goddess of dawn). Ritam = the truth and order of the universe. A famous idea: 'Ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti' — truth is one, the wise call it by many names. The last hymn of the Rig Veda says: 'walk together, speak together, let your minds be one'. The daily offering to Agni was the yajna.

What was Vedic society like?

People lived in janas (clans/tribes) — the Rig Veda names more than 30: Bharatas, Purus, Kurus, Yadus, Turvashas. Words that are still used today: raja, sabha, samiti. Occupations: farmer, weaver, potter, carpenter, physician, dancer, barber, priest.

The Upanishads — big questions, deep answers

The Upanishads brought new ideas — rebirth and karma (as you act, so you reap). Vedanta: everything is made of one divine reality, brahman; the same divine form in every living being = atman. 'Aham brahmasmi' (I am brahman), 'Tat tvam asi' (you are that), 'Sarve bhavantu sukhinah' (may all be happy). Three stories: Shvetaketu — proud even after 12 years at the gurukul, so his father Uddalaka Aruni used the tiny seed of a banyan tree to show that an unseen essence is in everything; Nachiketa — asked Yama (god of death) 'what happens after death?' — the answer: the atman is immortal; Gargi debated with Yajnavalkya in the court of King Janaka. Yoga — ways of experiencing brahman in one's own consciousness. Together these became the foundation of today's Hinduism.

Buddhism — the path to freedom from suffering

Siddhartha Gautama — born at Lumbini (Nepal), ~560 BCE (estimate). At the age of 29 he saw an old man, a sick man, a dead man and a calm ascetic outside the palace — and left the palace. At Bodh Gaya (Bihar) he gained enlightenment under a peepal tree → Buddha = 'the awakened one'. The root of suffering: avidya (ignorance) and attachment. Ahimsa = not hurting anyone. The community of monks and nuns = the Sangha. Jataka tales — the earlier births of the Buddha (the monkey-king story, Bharhut).

Jainism — three precious principles

Vardhamana — born near Vaishali (Bihar), early 6th century BCE. He left home at 30, did 12 years of penance → 'infinite knowledge' → Mahavira ('great hero'). 'Jain' comes from jina (conqueror — one who conquers ignorance and attachment). Principles: Ahimsa (non-violence), Anekantavada (truth has many sides — like the story of the blind men and the elephant!), Aparigraha (not hoarding more than you need). A story: the thief Rohineya changed his ways — everyone deserves a second chance. At Ellora (6th–10th century CE), caves of Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions stand side by side!

A different school of thought and the shared 'trunk'

Charvaka/Lokayata — only the material world is real, nothing exists after death; it did not become popular. All the others share a common trunk: dharma, karma, rebirth, and freedom from suffering and ignorance.

Tribal communities — part of our roots

2011: 705 tribes, ~10.4 crore people (more than the population of Australia + the UK!). Ancient India had no word 'tribe' — they too were janas; the Constitution uses 'Scheduled Tribes' (janjati). Give and take: Jagannath (Puri) was originally a tribal deity; the Toda (Nilgiris) hold 30+ peaks sacred and do not point a finger at them; Donyipolo (Arunachal — Sun + Moon), Khandoba, Singbonga (Munda, Santhal). Sociologist André Béteille: Hindu and tribal religions influenced each other.

The family — society's first 'brick'

Grassroots Democracy – Part 1: Governance

Why we need rules, what a government is, its 3 organs and 3 levels, and the meaning of democracy.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Why do we need rules?

Imagine everyone in class talking at once, or everyone driving wherever they like on the road — chaos! When many people live together, rules are necessary — at home, at school, on the road, in the office, everywhere. Governance = taking decisions, making rules and getting them followed. The system that does this work = the Government. The big, important rules = laws — and they can be changed when needed.

An example — cybercrime

Online fraud (cybercrime) was a new crime. So first a new law was made, then the cyber police caught the criminal, and the court gave the punishment. Here all three organs of the government were at work!

The 3 organs of government — 3 different jobs

The Legislature — makes and changes laws; the representatives chosen by the people. The Executive — implements laws; the head of state, ministers, police. The Judiciary — the courts; decides whether a law was broken and gives punishment; examines the decisions of the executive and the laws themselves. Why are they separate? So that no one organ becomes too powerful — separation of powers → checks and balances (each organ keeps watch on the others), like the players, the umpire and the third umpire in cricket!

Three levels — from village to nation

Local (village/city), State, Central/Union. A flood example: a small flood → local government; a bigger one → State government; a very large one → the Centre (army, relief). Work of the Centre: defence, foreign policy, atomic energy, communications, currency, trade between states, education, national policies. Work of the State: police/law and order, implementing the Centre's laws, health, education, agriculture, irrigation, local government.

Who is who? — nation and state

Nation: Judiciary — the Supreme Court; Legislature — Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha; Executive — the President (nominal head, Supreme Commander of the armed forces) and the Prime Minister (the real executive head). State: the High Court; the Vidhan Sabha (one House in most states, two in some); the Governor (nominal) and the Chief Minister (the real head). Mottos: Government of India — 'Satyameva Jayate' (truth alone triumphs); Supreme Court — 'Yato Dharmastato Jayah' (where there is dharma, there is victory).

Lessons from Kalam Sir

Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam — born in 1931, Rameswaram (TN); 'Missile Man of India'; 11th President (2002–2007). His fun lesson: F.A.I.L. = First Attempt In Learning, E.N.D. = Effort Never Dies, N.O. = Next Opportunity. In other words, don't be afraid of failure!

Democracy — rule of the people

Democracy = Greek demos (people) + kratos (rule) = 'rule of the people'. Crores of people cannot all rule directly, so we choose representatives — representative democracy: an MLA for the state, an MP for the nation. India = the world's largest democracy — about 97 crore voters in 2024; citizens aged 18+ can vote. Direct democracy — everyone votes directly (like deciding the class picnic by raising hands!). Grassroots democracy = participation of ordinary people — the base of the pyramid of democracy.

Grassroots Democracy – Part 2: Local Government in Rural Areas

Panchayati Raj — the village's own government, a three-tier system.

The real India lives in its villages

Gandhiji said — 'The real India lives in its villages'. India has about 6 lakh villages, about 8,000 towns and over 4,000 cities; out of 1.4 billion people, about two-thirds live in villages. Our example village is Lakshmanpur (in the foothills of the Himalayas) — 200 houses, about 700 people. It is hard to run so many villages from Delhi, so every village has its own small 'government' — the Panchayat.

Panchayati Raj — our own rule, in our own hands

Panchayat = the village council. Panchayati Raj = self-government — government close to the people, with schemes reaching right down to the ground. It is like a three-storey building (from the bottom up): Gram Panchayat (village) → Panchayat Samiti / Block Panchayat / Mandal Parishad (block) → Zila Parishad (district).

Gram Sabha and Gram Panchayat — know the difference

The Gram Sabha = all the registered adult voters of a village (or a few nearby villages) — in other words, all the grown-ups of the village! The Gram Sabha elects the members of the Gram Panchayat. The head of the Panchayat = the Sarpanch / Pradhan. Helpers: the Panchayat Secretary (calls meetings, keeps records) and the Patwari (land records, sometimes maps that are generations old). Think of it this way — the Gram Sabha is 'all the children in the class', the Gram Panchayat is 'the elected class committee'!

Inspiring Sarpanches

Dnyaneshwar Kamble — a transgender Sarpanch, Tarangfal (Solapur, Maharashtra), 2017; motto 'service to the people, service to the village'. Vandana Bahadur Maida — from the Bhil community, the first woman Sarpanch of Khankhandvi (MP). Popatrao Pawar — Hiware Bazar (Ahmednagar): following the Anna Hazare model, he held back rainwater, built watersheds and planted lakhs of trees → a dry village turned green; Padma Shri 2020.

Panchayats for children too!

Child-Friendly Panchayat: Bal Sabha, Bal Panchayat — in Maharashtra, children raise their voice against child labour and child marriage. In the Sangkhu Radhu Khandu GP (West Sikkim), a school wall and a kitchen were built at the children's request. Children's Parliament — Bunker Roy's Barefoot College (Rajasthan); children aged 8–14; World's Children's Honorary Award 2001. In other words, you too can take part in the decisions of your village or city!

The upper two tiers and special rules

The Panchayat Samiti = the link between the Gram Panchayat and the Zila Parishad; it joins the plans of many villages; schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana. At all three levels there are special rules for disadvantaged groups; 1/3 of the seats are reserved for women. The structure differs a little in every state, because Panchayati Raj is a state subject. Ancient history: in Kautilya's Arthashastra (~2,300 years ago) — for every 10 villages a sangrahana, for 100 a karvatika, for 400 a dronamukha, for 800 a sthaniya!

Grassroots Democracy – Part 3: Local Government in Urban Areas

Why does a city need its own 'government'?

Cities are far bigger and more complicated than villages — lakhs of people, roads, water pipelines, garbage, traffic. To manage all this we have Urban Local Bodies (local governments of cities), so that local people can put their views directly (decentralisation). In the Constituent Assembly, Rustom K. Sidhwa (13 Oct 1949) said that local bodies should be set up right away so that people learn about administration, voting and their rights. Participatory democracy = citizens take part in running the country at every level.

The oldest municipal corporations

Madras Corporation (now the Greater Chennai Corporation) — 29 September 1688 — is India's oldest civic body! The East India Company's charter of 1687 covered Fort St. George + 16 km; a law of 1792 gave it the right to collect taxes. The Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai (earlier the Bombay Municipal Corporation) — 1865.

City size — name of the body

By population: more than 10 lakh → Municipal Corporation; 1–10 lakh → Municipal Council; smaller towns → Nagar Panchayat. A city is divided into wards; each ward has a ward committee — health camps, campaigns against single-use plastic, complaints about leaking drains and roads. The pyramid: Corporation/Council/Nagar Panchayat → Ward Committee → people of the ward.

What does a municipal corporation do?

Infrastructure such as roads and bridges, cremation grounds and burial grounds, collecting and disposing of garbage, checking schemes, local taxes and fines, and planning for economic and social development. Services of the Indore Municipal Corporation: property tax, water bill, solid waste, licences, marriage certificates, fire brigade, water tankers, ambulance… Indore has been the cleanest city in the Swachh Survekshan for 7 years in a row!

Our duty too

The government cannot do everything alone. A citizen's tasks: separating garbage (wet and dry) — segregation, reporting at once if you see a water leak, and keeping public places clean. The conversation between Sameer and Anita: in a village everyone knows each other and even children get a hearing; a city is more complicated — but 'every voice matters'.

The Value of Work

Work is worship too

Swami Vivekananda: 'Do every work as if it were worship.' We do many kinds of work through the day — some for money, some out of love. In this chapter we will understand the value of both.

Economic vs non-economic — what is the difference?

Economic activity = work done in exchange for money or something worth money — a shopkeeper, a farmer selling a crop, a lawyer's fee, a truck driver, a factory worker. Non-economic activity = work done out of love, care, gratitude or respect, not for money — a mother cooking for the family, a brother helping with homework, looking after grandparents, helping with repairs at home.

The story of Anu and Kabir

Geeta Aunty — an Indian Air Force pilot, earns a salary → economic. Kabir's grandfather (retired BSF) teaches children geography free of cost → non-economic. Rohan — a software engineer all week (economic) and a volunteer on weekends (non-economic). So the same person can do both kinds of work!

The market and 4 ways of being paid

Market = a place where goods and services are exchanged, mostly for money. Ways of payment: Salary — fixed and regular, usually every month. Wage — payment for work over a fixed time (like a daily wage). Fee — payment for professional advice or service (doctor, lawyer, tuition). Payment in kind — payment in goods instead of money. The story of Kavya: her uncle is a bulldozer technician (salary), her aunt works at the post office (salary) + gives online tuition (fee), and Sahil is a farm labourer — a daily wage, part cash and part mangoes (payment in kind)!

Value addition — the carpenter's chair

Rajesh the carpenter bought wood for ₹600, made a chair and sold it for ₹1,000. The extra ₹400 = the value of his skill, time and effort — this is called value addition. At every stage the value of the product rises.

Work without pay — the most precious

Seva — the langar of the Golden Temple (lakhs of people eat free every day), the prasad at a temple. Swachh Bharat Abhiyan — cleaning together. Van Mahotsav — the festival of planting trees. Celebrating festivals together. Such work joins society together — its worth cannot be measured in money.

Primary, Secondary, Tertiary — the three sectors and how they depend on each other (the story of AMUL).

Put all work into three 'boxes'

Kautilya (Arthashastra): 'The root of prosperity is economic activity.' To make sense of the thousands of kinds of work in the world, we group them by similarity into three sectors: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary. Think of the journey of a chapati: growing wheat (primary) → grinding flour (secondary) → getting it to the shop and selling it (tertiary)!

Primary sector — straight from nature

Work that takes things directly from nature: farming, mining, fishing, animal rearing, forestry, poultry (eggs). Greenhouse farming too.

Secondary sector — change and make

Giving a new form to primary products by changing them: manufacturing, construction (buildings, roads), utilities (water, electricity, gas), solar power. Examples: a flour mill, oil from groundnuts, tea leaves, furniture and paper from wood, cloth from cotton, iron → steel → cars. India in 2022 (SIAM): about 45 lakh passenger vehicles, 10.3 lakh commercial vehicles, 8.6 lakh three-wheelers and 2 crore two-wheelers!

Tertiary sector — services that support

Services that support the first two sectors: transport, banks, trade, shops (retail), hospitals, teaching, communication, software, hotels, warehouses, repair. That is why it is also called the service sector.

AMUL — the farmers' own victory

AMUL = Anand Milk Union Limited, Anand district (Gujarat). In the 1940s milk-producing farmers were troubled by middlemen — they got low prices. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel advised them to form a cooperative. AMUL was founded in 1946 — led by Tribhuvandas Patel (a lawyer and freedom fighter) and Dr. Verghese Kurien (an engineer). Farmers (including women) gained control. Pasteurisation = heating milk to a set temperature to kill bacteria. Then came butter, milk powder, ghee, cheese — and exports too!

All three sectors together in AMUL

Milking cows and buffaloes → primary; making butter and powder → secondary; transport, trade and shops → tertiary. Other milk cooperatives: Nandini (Karnataka), Mother Dairy (Delhi-NCR), Aavin (Tamil Nadu), Vijaya (Andhra), Kevi (Nagaland), Sudha (Bihar), Verka (Punjab).

The journey of a book, and recycling

Your book: pulp from trees (primary) → paper and printing (secondary) → transport and shops (tertiary). The magic of recycling: recycle 1 tonne of paper and you save 17 trees, save 2.5 cubic metres of landfill (garbage dump) space, and use 70% less energy and water. Give old notebooks as scrap — don't throw them away!

C. Short-answer (model answers)

  1. 1What is the difference between local time and standard time?

    Local time is fixed by the longitude of each place — it is noon when the sun is overhead. Having many local times in one country is inconvenient, so a country picks one meridian and adopts a standard time. In India this is IST, which is 5:30 hours ahead of GMT. So even though Gujarat and Assam have different local times, clocks in both show the same time (IST).

  2. 2Why does the Harappan civilisation have many names?

    Harappan — after Harappa, the first city to be excavated (1920-21). Indus — at first, most sites were found in the Indus plains. Indus-Sarasvati / Sindhu-Sarasvati — later, many sites (Rakhigarhi, Ganweriwala, Kalibangan) were found in the Sarasvati (Ghaggar-Hakra) basin. 'Indus Valley' is now obsolete, because the civilisation spread up to Gujarat and Haryana.

  3. 3Write the main ideas of Buddhism.

    The cause of suffering is avidya (ignorance) and attachment (moh); the Buddha showed the path to overcome them. Ahimsa — not hurting anyone, even in thought. True inner discipline: 'No one becomes pure by water; one who has truth and dharma is pure.' Founding of the Sangha — monks and nuns spread the teachings.

  4. 4How does the sari show 'unity in diversity'?

    The sari is one kind of unstitched cloth (unity), worn across almost all of India. But the fabric (cotton, silk), weave, design, colour and way of wearing differ in each region — Banarasi, Kanjivaram, Paithani, Patola, Muga, Mysore (diversity). Women have also found many uses for it. A relief at Vaishali shows that it has been worn since a few centuries BCE.

  5. 5Why do we need three levels of government?

    Not every problem can be solved from one place. Small, local matters (village road, drain) are handled by the local government; problems of several districts by the State; and subjects of the whole country (defence, currency, foreign policy) or a huge disaster by the Central government. Example of a flood: a small flood is handled locally, a bigger one by the State, and in a huge one the Centre sends the army and relief.

  6. 6Describe the three levels of Panchayati Raj and their work.

    Gram Panchayat (village) — elected by the Gram Sabha; manages village work under the Sarpanch/Pradhan. Panchayat Samiti (block) — the link between the Gram Panchayats and the Zila Parishad; joins all their development plans and brings funds and schemes (PMGSY). Zila Parishad (district) — planning and coordination for the whole district. In all three, 1/3 of seats are for women.

  7. 7Explain the difference between economic and non-economic activities with examples.

    An economic activity is done in return for money or money's worth — Geeta Aunty's job as a pilot (salary), Sahil's labour (wage + kind), a doctor's fee. A non-economic activity is done out of love, care or service — parents cooking food, a grandfather teaching for free, a langar. Both matter: one earns an income, the other binds society and family together.

  8. 8Explain the interdependence of the three sectors with the example of AMUL.

    Farmers get milk from cows and buffaloes — primary. The milk is pasteurised and made into butter, ghee, cheese and milk powder — secondary. Transport by truck, rail and air, and sale in retail shops — tertiary. If any one sector stops, the whole chain stops: there is milk but no factory, or there is a product but no transport.
