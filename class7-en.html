Geographical Diversity of India

From the Himalaya to the islands — India's five major geographical regions and how they shape life.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

'Better than the whole world' — how does India look from above?

In 1984, PM Indira Gandhi asked Rakesh Sharma — the first Indian in space — how India looks from above. His answer: 'Saare jahan se achchha!' (better than the whole world). India is the 7th largest country in the world. Together with Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and Myanmar, it forms the Indian Subcontinent. It has 5 major regions: mountains, plains, desert, the southern plateau and islands. Natural boundaries: the Himalaya in the north, the Thar and the Arabian Sea in the west, the Indian Ocean in the south, and the Bay of Bengal in the east.

How did the Himalaya form? — the carpet example

Millions of years ago, India was part of a huge landmass called Gondwana — and its neighbour was Africa! Slowly drifting, India crashed into Asia (Eurasia) about 5 crore (50 million) years ago. Imagine pushing a carpet on the floor from both sides — folds rise in the middle. In the same way, the land crumpled and folded to form the Himalaya! Even today India is pushing into Asia by about 5 cm every year, and the Himalaya is rising by about 5 mm every year. 'Himalaya' = hima (snow) + alaya (home) = the home of snow.

The Himalaya — Asia's water tank

The Himalaya is about 2,500 km long and spread over 6 countries (India, Nepal, Bhutan, China, Pakistan, Afghanistan); many peaks are higher than 8,000 m — the 'Eight Thousanders'. Its melting snow feeds the Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra — that is why it is called the 'Water Tower of Asia'. The main stream of the Ganga, the Bhagirathi, begins at Gaumukh (Gangotri glacier). It has three ranges: Himadri (the highest — Everest, Kanchenjunga, snow all year), Himachal (the middle one — Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital, Darjeeling), Shivalik (the lowest — dense forests). The kath-kuni houses (stone + wood) of the western Himalaya stay warm and can withstand mild earthquakes. Ladakh is a cold desert, with winters of −30°C; Pangong Tso is a salty lake; the yak gives milk, wool and rides, all in one!

The northern plains — a gift of soil

The Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra carry soil down from the mountains and spread it across the plains — that is why this land is very fertile, heavily populated, and, being flat, easy for building roads and railways. Brahmaputra = 'son of Brahma' — one of the few rivers given a male name; it swells in summer when snow melts. Wildlife: langurs, tigers (Project Tiger), the long-snouted gharial (on the brink of extinction), and the peacock (national bird).

The Thar desert and the ancient Aravalli

The Thar covers Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab and Haryana; its sand dunes rise up to 150 m! Jaisalmer is the 'Golden City'; Pushkar has its famous fair; to save water, people build a taanka/kund (an underground tank). The Aravalli is among the oldest mountains in the world (about 2.5 billion years); worn down over time, they are now low; the highest peak is Mount Abu; they stop the Thar from spreading east. In the Zawar mines here, over 800 years ago, Indians became the first in the world to extract zinc!

The southern plateau and the two Ghats

The Peninsular Plateau has sea on three sides. In the west are the Western Ghats (high, wall-like, UNESCO site; the northern part is the Sahyadri), and in the east the Eastern Ghats (lower, broken). In between lies the Deccan Plateau, tilted towards the east — so most rivers flow east: Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi → Bay of Bengal. Only the Narmada and Tapti flow west → Arabian Sea. In the forests live the Santhal, Gond, Baiga, Bhil and Korku tribes.

Coasts and islands

The coastline is more than 7,500 km long. The west coast is narrow, with fast rivers and estuaries (Narmada, Tapti). The east coast is broad, with deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) and the Chilika and Pulicat lagoons. Lakshadweep has 36 coral islands. The Andaman & Nicobar islands are 500+ islands formed by volcanoes; there is the Cellular Jail; Barren Island is India's only active volcano. The Sundarbans — the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta, mangrove forest, home of the Royal Bengal Tiger. In the north-east: the Garo-Khasi-Jaintia hills; Mawlynnong — 'the cleanest village in Asia'; living root bridges made from tree roots!

Understanding the Weather

The 5 elements of weather, the instruments that measure them, weather stations and forecasts.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

What is weather?

Sunny today, rainy tomorrow — this is weather: the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time. The atmosphere is the blanket of gases around the Earth (the 'air'). The lowest layer is the troposphere — we live here and almost all weather happens here; it is 6–18 km high (thin at the poles, thick near the equator). Above it is the stratosphere — where the ozone layer protects us from the Sun's harmful rays.

The 5 elements of weather

(1) Temperature (hot or cold), (2) Precipitation (rain, snow, hail, sleet), (3) Atmospheric pressure (the weight of air), (4) Wind (the speed and direction of air), (5) Humidity (water vapour or moisture in the air). The scientific study of weather = meteorology. People in the past also read signs: ants carrying eggs to higher ground, frogs croaking loudly, pine cones closing → rain is coming!

Measuring heat — the thermometer

A thermometer measures temperature; the unit is Celsius or Fahrenheit (15°C = 59°F). The day's range = highest − lowest. The mean daily temperature = (max + min) ÷ 2. Example: max 30°C, min 20°C → mean 25°C, range 10°C.

Measuring rain — the rain gauge

A rain gauge = funnel + cylinder + scale. If 5 mm of water collects in the cylinder, it was 5 mm of rain. You can even make one at home with a bottle and a funnel! The India Meteorological Department (IMD) — since 1875; its motto is 'adityat jayate vrishti' — 'rain comes from the Sun'.

Air pressure — the barometer

Atmospheric pressure = the weight of the column of air above us. It is higher at the seashore and lower on mountains — that is why there is less oxygen on mountains and people get breathless. It is measured with a barometer, in millibars (mb). At sea level it is about 1013 mb; below 1000 mb = a 'depression' / low pressure → a sign of a storm or cyclone. At Khardung La (Ladakh, 5,600 m+) it is only about 650 mb!

Wind speed, direction and moisture

Wind always moves from high pressure to low pressure — like air rushing out of a balloon. Direction is shown by a wind vane (at airports, a cloth wind sock); speed is measured by an anemometer (3–4 cups that spin), in km/h. Humidity is given as relative humidity %: dry 20–40%, moist 60–80%. In high humidity clothes dry slowly and we sweat more. The instrument: hygrometer. In summer, the hot, dusty wind of north India = Loo.

The weather station and warning colours

All the instruments in one place = a weather station. An Automated Weather Station (AWS) records by itself using sensors; in 2023 the NDMA installed an AWS at 4,800 m+ on a glacial lake in Sikkim. IMD's warnings: Watch (stay informed), Alert (be prepared), Warning (take action). Because of forecasts, fishermen don't go out to sea, and coasts are evacuated before a cyclone — lives are saved!

Climates of India

Weather vs climate, the types of climate in India, their causes, the monsoon, disasters and climate change.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Weather, season, climate — three different things

Weather = today's conditions (changes every day). Season (ritu) = a period of the year that returns every year (summer, rainy season, winter). Climate = the long-term (at least 30 years) average pattern of a place. Think of it like this: your mood today = weather, your nature = climate! India's traditional 6 seasons: Vasanta, Grishma, Varsha, Sharad, Hemanta, Shishir — with festivals too: Vasanta Panchami, Sharad Purnima.

How many types of climate are there in India?

The Himalaya — alpine (very cold); the lower hills — temperate (hill stations); the northern plains — subtropical (wheat); the Thar — arid (dry); the west coast — tropical wet (rice, spices); the Deccan — semi-arid; the south-east — tropical. One country, many climates!

What decides the climate? — 5 factors

(1) Latitude — at the equator the Sun's rays fall straight (heat is concentrated), at the poles they fall slanting (heat is spread out) → Kanniyakumari is hot, Srinagar is cold. (2) Altitude (height) — the higher you go, the thinner and colder the air: Ooty (10–25°C) vs Coimbatore (25–38°C) — almost the same latitude! (3) Nearness to the sea — the sea keeps temperatures balanced: Mumbai (32°/18°, range ~14°) vs Nagpur (44°/10°, range 34°). (4) Winds — in summer hot wind from Arabia-Afghanistan → heat wave in Punjab-Rajasthan; in winter cold wind from beyond the Himalaya → cold wave. (5) Topography — the Himalaya-Karakoram block the cold winds of Central Asia. A small area's own climate = microclimate; a concrete city that is warmer than its surroundings = urban heat island.

Monsoon — the game of winds that reverse

Monsoon comes from the Arabic word 'mausim' (season). In summer, land heats up faster than the sea → low pressure over land → moisture-laden wind flows from the sea to the land → rain! In winter it is the opposite. South-west monsoon: arrives in Kerala in early June and covers the whole country by mid-July; the western slopes of the Western Ghats get heavy rain, and the Deccan behind them gets little (rain shadow). North-east monsoon (winter): picks up moisture from the Bay of Bengal and brings rain to areas like Tamil Nadu. Mawsynram (Meghalaya) has the highest average rainfall in the world, about 11,000 mm (11 metres!). The raga Megh Malhar is the raga of rain.

Signs from nature and monsoon 'failure'

Konkan fishermen predict rain by seeing fish near the surface; the monsoon comes about 50 days after the Golden Shower (amaltas) blooms; a crow's nest built high → less rain. If the monsoon fails: crops are ruined, water runs short, people migrate, and prices rise (inflation).

Disasters — cyclones, floods, landslides, fires

A cyclone is a low-pressure system with fast-spinning winds; the calm, cloud-free centre = the 'eye of the cyclone'; it hits the east coast more often; the NDRF has 12 battalions. Floods — UP, Bihar, Kerala, Andhra, Assam; in the Himalaya, glacial lakes burst; the 2013 Uttarakhand (Kedarnath) disaster — about 6,000 deaths; in cities, floods come from poor drainage and encroachment. Landslides — Himachal, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal, Western Ghats; caused by cutting forests and wrong construction. Forest fires — Uttarakhand, Himachal, MP, Chhattisgarh.

Climate change — a fever for the Earth

Since the 19th century, humans have been burning coal, petrol and gas (fossil fuels) and cutting down forests. This increases greenhouse gases (like CO₂), which trap heat like a blanket → global warming. In early 2025 India's temperature was 1–3°C above normal. The remedy (mitigation): less pollution, planting trees, and renewable energy like solar and wind.

New Beginnings: Cities and States

The Second Urbanisation — janapadas, the 16 mahajanapadas, gana-sanghas, iron, coins and the varna-jati system.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Cities vanished, then returned — the 'Second Urbanisation'

The cities of Harappa (the First Urbanisation) declined in the early 2nd millennium BCE, and for about 1,000 years no big cities were seen at all. Then, in the 1st millennium BCE, new cities rose in the Ganga plains and nearby areas — the Second Urbanisation — which continues even today! Our information comes from excavations and from late Vedic, Buddhist and Jain texts.

From Janapada to Mahajanapada

Janapada = 'the place where the jana (people) set their pada (foot)' — the territory of a clan, led by a raja. By the 8th–7th century BCE, janapadas joined to form larger Mahajanapadas — the most popular list has 16: Gandhara in the north-west, Anga in the east, Ashmaka near the Godavari… The most powerful: Magadha (capital Rajagriha — today's Rajgir, Bihar), Kosala (Shravasti), Vatsa (Kaushambi), Avanti (Ujjayini). Many of these capitals are still living cities today — about 2,500 years old!

Why the Ganga plains?

Fertile land (plenty of grain), iron ore in the nearby hills, and new trade routes. Around the capitals were earth or stone walls (ramparts), a moat full of water outside, and narrow gateways so that entry and exit could be controlled.

Kings, but republics too

Every janapada had a sabha/samiti — an assembly that advised the elders; according to some texts, an unfit king could be removed. Most mahajanapadas were monarchies (kings from generation to generation; taxes, laws, forts, armies). But Vajji (Vrijji) and Malla were gana/sangha — assemblies that took decisions by discussion and voting, and even chose their ruler! These were among the earliest republics in the world.

The age of iron and the first coins

Harappa = copper/bronze; Second Urbanisation = iron. Iron tools meant larger-scale farming and faster, lighter weapons. India's first coins were silver punch-marked coins (symbols stamped with a punch); later came copper and gold; each mahajanapada had its own coins.

Jati and varna — the structure of society

Jati = a community with a particular occupation — skills passed down generations, with their own customs. Varna (from the Vedic texts) = 4: Brahmin (knowledge, rituals), Kshatriya (protection), Vaishya (trade, farming), Shudra (artisans, labourers). The word 'caste' comes from the Portuguese 'casta' (16th century). At first the system was flexible — people changed occupations; over time, especially under British rule, it became rigid and discrimination grew.

Routes and cities of the south-east

Two great routes: the Uttarapatha (north-west → Ganga plains → east) and the Dakshinapatha (from Kaushambi across the Vindhya to the south). Shishupalgarh (Bhubaneswar; excavated in 1948) — the capital of Kalinga, with a square plan. In the south, cities appeared from about 400 BCE; Chola, Chera, Pandya. Kodumanal (Erode, TN) — a shell and gemstone industry. By 300–200 BCE the whole subcontinent was connected and the age of the mahajanapadas ended.

The Rise of Empires

Magadha, the Nandas, Alexander, the Mauryas — Chandragupta and Kautilya, Ashoka, and the weakness of empires.

What is an empire?

Empire comes from the Latin 'imperium' (supreme power). When one powerful king (an emperor) rules over many smaller kingdoms, and the smaller kings pay him tribute (gifts or taxes), that is an empire. In Sanskrit: samraj, adhiraja, rajadhiraja. An empire needs an army, an administration, a network of roads, rivers and sea routes, common laws, coins and measures, and support for art and learning. Traders' and craftspeople's guilds (shreni) made their own rules — the king did not interfere.

Magadha — a treasure house of strength

Magadha (south Bihar) became the most powerful kingdom between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE because of: the fertile Ganga plains, forests (timber and elephants — the tanks of the army!), iron nearby, and the Ganga and Son rivers. King Ajatashatru lived in the time of the Buddha and Mahavira. Then came Mahapadma Nanda — founder of the Nanda dynasty, with a big army; the last king, Dhana Nanda, was rich but oppressed the people. In this same period, Panini wrote the Ashtadhyayi — 3,996 sutras of Sanskrit grammar!

Alexander and Porus

Alexander of Macedonia conquered the Persian empire and came to India in 327–325 BCE, where he defeated King Porus (Paurava) in Punjab. When he asked, 'How should I treat you?', Porus replied — 'as one king treats another'! Impressed, Alexander made him a satrap (governor) and gave back his kingdom. His soldiers were not ready to march towards the Ganga; Alexander turned back and died in Babylon at the age of 32. He was also amazed by the calm answers of the Indian 'gymnosophists' (naked philosophers).

The pair of Chandragupta and Chanakya

Chanakya (Kautilya/Vishnugupta) was a teacher at Takshashila. Dhana Nanda insulted him, so he took a vow to end Nanda rule. Together with the young Chandragupta, around 321 BCE, he founded the Maurya empire; its capital was Pataliputra (Patna). Chandragupta defeated the Greek satraps; the Greek ambassador Megasthenes came to his court and wrote 'Indika' — two harvests a year, few famines, farmers safe even during war, two-storey wooden houses, and water vessels kept ready for fires.

Arthashastra — a book on how to rule

Kautilya's Arthashastra = the science of governance and the economy. The state has 7 limbs (Saptanga): Swami (the king), Amatya (the minister), Janapada (territory + people), Durga (the fortified city), Kosha (the treasury), Danda (army and law), Mitra (friendly states). The biggest lesson: 'in the happiness of the people lies the happiness of the king' — the people's welfare comes first.

Ashoka — from the sword to peace

Ashoka (268–232 BCE) was Chandragupta's grandson. Seeing the death of lakhs of people in the Kalinga (Odisha) war, he was deeply saddened and gave up violence; he adopted the Buddha's path of peace. He sent envoys to Sri Lanka, Thailand and Central Asia. His messages (edicts) were carved on rocks and pillars — in the Prakrit language and Brahmi script (the 'mother' of Indian scripts). His title: 'Devanampiya Piyadasi' (beloved of the gods, looking kindly on all). His works: treatment for humans and animals, limits on hunting, wells, rest houses and trees along roads, respect for all sects, and a tour by officials every 5 years to check on justice. The Sohgaura copper plate — a granary for famine.

The Lions of Sarnath — our national emblem

The Sarnath Lion Capital has 4 lions (royal power), and below them an elephant, bull, horse, lion and the dharmachakra. This is our national emblem; its motto is 'Satyameva Jayate' (from the Mundaka Upanishad); and the wheel sits in the middle of the tricolour! Also: the Sanchi stupa (the round part = the egg or anda; walking around it = pradakshina), the stone elephant at Dhauli, and the Barabar caves. The Mauryas lasted until about 185 BCE. Why do empires fall? Unhappiness over heavy taxes, weak successors, an area that is too large, and disasters — empires that rest on war are fragile.

The Age of Reorganisation

After the Mauryas (c. 185 BCE – 3rd century CE): the Shungas, Satavahanas, Chedis, the three kingdoms of the Sangam age, Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas.

Why an age of 'reorganisation'?

Around 185 BCE, the last Maurya emperor was killed by his commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga. The big empire broke up into many new kingdoms — like a big book whose pages scatter and form several small books. That is why this is called the 'Age of Reorganisation'. There were two ways of expanding a kingdom: alliances through marriage or war. Art, architecture and literature flourished in this period.

The Shungas and the Satavahanas

Shungas: Pushyamitra performed the ashvamedha yajna (a horse was let loose — wherever it roamed unchecked, that land belonged to the king). Patanjali compiled the Yoga Sutras around this time. The Bharhut stupa has a beautifully carved railing. The Satavahanas ('Andhras') ruled the Deccan from the 2nd century BCE; their capitals were Amaravati and Pratishthana (Paithan); their coins show a ship with two masts → trade up to Rome (spices, cloth, sandalwood, pearls). Their kings were known by their mothers' names — Gautamiputra Satakarni (mother Gautami Balashri, Nashik inscription). Some of the Brahmi numerals in the Naneghat caves look like today's numerals — meaning the roots of today's numerals lie in India! The Karla caves, and the Pitalkhora yaksha made by the goldsmith Kanhadasa.

Kharavela — the 'monk-king'

Kharavela, king of the Chedi dynasty (Kalinga), was a devotee of Jainism. The Udayagiri-Khandagiri caves (Bhubaneswar). The Hathigumpha inscription records his deeds year by year: 'respect for every sect, repair of every temple'.

The south's 'three crowned kings' and the Sangam

The Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas were independent even in Ashoka's time. A collection of their poems = Sangam literature (sangam = assembly) — about love, valour and generosity. The Chola king Karikala built the Kallanai (Grand Anicut) on the Kaveri — still in use after ~2,000 years! Because of it, the Kaveri delta is the 'rice bowl of the South'. The Silappadikaram ('the story of the anklet'): Kannagi's husband Kovalan was falsely accused of theft in Madurai and put to death; Kannagi proved the truth with her second anklet — the message: a king must deliver justice. The Cheras (Vanji/Karur) — trade with Rome; the Pandyas (Madurai) — famous for pearls.

People from outside — Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas

The Indo-Greeks were descendants of Alexander's satraps. At the Heliodorus pillar (Vidisha), a Greek ambassador called Vasudeva (Krishna) the 'God of gods'; the three immortal rules: self-restraint, charity, awareness. The Shakas — the Shaka Samvat is 78 years behind the Gregorian calendar; in 1957 it became the Indian National Calendar. The Kushanas — Kanishka was the most powerful ('maharaja rajadhiraja devaputra'); their coins show the Buddha and Shiva with Nandi; they controlled the Silk Route. The people who came from outside blended into the local culture!

Two schools of sculpture

Gandhara — a blend of Greco-Roman and Indian styles; grey-black stone (schist); a lifelike body and flowing robes. Mathura — a local style; red sandstone; full, rounded forms (Kubera's moustache!). In this same period Sanskrit literature flourished; the composition of the Mahabharata and Ramayana continued.

The Gupta Era: An Age of Tireless Creativity

The Gupta empire (3rd–6th century CE) — the 'classical age': science, literature and art; along with the Vakatakas, Pallavas and Kamarupa.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Why is the Gupta period called 'golden'?

Imagine a long period of peace — people were happy, trade grew, and art and science flourished. The Gupta period was just like that, which is why it is called the 'classical age'. There were other kingdoms at the time too: the Vakatakas (capital Nandivardhana, near Nagpur), the Pallavas (Kanchipuram) and Kamarupa (Assam). One interesting piece of evidence: the Iron Pillar of Mehrauli in Delhi — over 1,600 years old, 6 tonnes, and still not rusted! The secret: a thin protective layer formed by oxygen. It belongs to the time of Chandragupta II.

Three great Gupta kings

Chandragupta I — coins and alliances; his coins show Queen Kumaradevi, with Lakshmi on the back. Samudragupta — praised in the Prayaga Prashasti (Prayagraj pillar) by the poet Harisena; his aim was 'dharani-bandha' (to unite the earth); he often returned the throne to defeated kings after taking tribute; his coins show him playing the veena! Chandragupta II 'Vikramaditya' — a devotee of Vishnu (Garuda emblem); his court had scholars and poets.

Prabhavati Gupta — 'mother of two kings'

Prabhavati, the daughter of Chandragupta II, was married to a Vakataka prince (an alliance through marriage). After her husband's death she became regent (one who rules on behalf of young sons) and had 7 temples of Vishnu built (Ramagiri/Ramtek, Maharashtra).

Administration and trade

The kingdom was divided into provinces; land grants were recorded on copper plates. The main income was the land tax; there were also fines, mines, irrigation and trade taxes. Trade reached the Mediterranean, South-East Asia and China — cloth, spices, ivory, gems. A Brahmi inscription on the island of Socotra in the Arabian Sea shows that Indian traders went that far! Though devotees of Vishnu, the Guptas supported Nalanda and Buddhist monasteries — a broad-minded outlook. The Chinese traveller Faxian (Fa-Hien) wrote that people were happy, cities rich, and that there were charity and free dispensaries; but he also wrote of harsh treatment of the chandalas — so for history we should look at many sources.

Aryabhata — the Earth rotates!

Aryabhata (~500 CE, Kusumapura, near Patna) wrote the Aryabhatiya. He explained that the Earth rotates on its axis, which causes day and night; the length of a year is about 365 days 6 hours 12 minutes (today's value is 365 d 5 h 48 m); the correct cause of eclipses; and the size of the Earth. Varahamihira (Ujjayini) wrote the Brihat Samhita: astronomy, weather, architecture, town planning, farming.

Poets, physicians and artists

Kalidasa wrote the Meghadutam ('the cloud messenger'): a yaksha sends a message to his beloved through a cloud, with a beautiful description of the landscape of North India along the way; also the Raghuvansham. The Ayurveda texts Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita took their final form — diseases, treatment, diet, surgery; the link between mind, body and nature. Art: the Buddha images of Sarnath, the paintings of Ajanta (the Bodhisattva Padmapani), the Udayagiri (MP) caves, the Deogarh Dashavatara temple (Vishnu on Sheshnaga), and the terracotta of Ahichchhatra (Ganga on a makara, Yamuna on a tortoise).

The end, and the kingdoms of the south-east

In the 6th century CE the Guptas weakened because of attacks by the Huns from Central Asia and the rise of regional kings. The Pallavas — devotees of Shiva; Kanchipuram = the 'city of a thousand temples'; ghatikas (centres of learning). Kamarupa — the Brahmaputra valley, earlier called Pragjyotisha. Samudragupta defeated the Pallavas and Kamarupa but did not rule them directly — he took tribute and returned the throne.

How the Land Becomes Sacred

Tirthas, pilgrimage and sacred geography — how the whole of India became a sacred land.

How does land become 'sacred'?

Some places, paths, rivers, mountains or trees have a very deep religious and spiritual importance for us — this is called sacredness. In India every religion has its sacred places: the Sanchi stupa, Mahabodhi (Bodh Gaya) — over 4 million (40 lakh+) pilgrims a year; the Sikh takhts — Patna Sahib, the Akal Takht (Amritsar), Keshgarh Sahib (Anandpur); the Ajmer Dargah and the Velankanni Church (TN) — which people of other faiths visit too. Guru Nanak made journeys to Haridwar, Prayag, Mathura, Varanasi, Ayodhya and Puri.

Tirtha — a place for 'crossing over'

Tirtha originally means a place where a river can be crossed; symbolically, it means crossing from ordinary life to spiritual life. Tirthayatra is a tradition about 3,000 years old — both an outer journey and an inner one. Pilgrims from villages near Lucknow used to walk for 3 months from Rameswaram to Haridwar! Sites linked with the Jain Tirthankaras ('makers of the crossing'): Mount Abu, Girnar, Shatrunjaya. Sabarimala (Kerala, Ayyappa) — over 10 million (1 crore+) a year; the hard hill path stands for the difficulty of the inner path. The Pandharpur Wari — 800 years old, 21 days on foot, to the Vithoba temple.

The whole country as a 'sacred network'

The Char Dham in all four directions; the 12 Jyotirlingas; the 51 Shakti Peethas (as far as Bangladesh and Pakistan) — the story: wherever the body parts of the goddess Sati fell, a peetha arose → the whole earth is the body of the Mother Goddess! On pilgrimage, people saw different languages, foods and clothes, yet also felt a sense of sameness; trade, debate and education went along too → cultural unity. Nehru (1961): journeys from Badrinath-Kedarnath to Kanyakumari gave a feeling of 'one country, one culture'. Pilgrim routes and trade routes were often the same — the Uttarapatha and Dakshinapatha.

Rivers and the Kumbh

The Rig Veda's Nadistuti Sukta praises 19 rivers. The mantra 'gange cha yamune chaiva…' names 7 rivers: Ganga, Yamuna, Godavari, Sarasvati, Narmada, Sindhu, Kaveri. The Kumbh Mela — at Prayagraj, the confluence (sangam) of the Ganga, the Yamuna and the unseen Sarasvati; a UNESCO intangible heritage; in 2025, about 66 crore (660 million) people! The story: the pot of nectar from the churning of the ocean; drops fell at 4 places — Haridwar, Prayagraj, Nashik, Ujjain.

Mountains, trees and sacred forests

Mountains = the gateway from earth to heaven: Kailash, Vaishno Devi, Tiruvannamalai, Tirumala. The peepal is sacred to Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains alike; peepal leaves even appear on a Mohenjo-daro seal; the Bodhi tree of Bodh Gaya. Sacred groves are forests of a deity where hunting and felling are forbidden → they protect biodiversity and water. Names: kavu (Malayalam), devarai (Marathi), oran (Rajasthan), sarna (Jharkhand), dev van (Himachal), Mawphlong (Meghalaya)… In a grove at Thanjavur, the deity protects fruit bats — which spread pollen and seeds! Niyam Dongar (Odisha) — the god of the Dongria Khond, Niyam Raja; felling trees is forbidden. Bhudevi (Mother Earth).

Around the world, and our responsibility today

Greece, the Native Americans, and the Maori of New Zealand — the law gave the mountain Taranaki Maunga rights like those of a person! But today our sacred rivers — the Yamuna, Mahanadi, Kaveri — are polluted. The Constitution also asks us to protect our heritage. David Suzuki: 'We treat the world the way we see it' — if you call the river a mother, do not make her dirty!

Theme D · Governance and Democracy

From the Rulers to the Ruled: Types of Governments

The work of a government, the types of democracy (direct, parliamentary, presidential) and monarchy, theocracy, dictatorship and oligarchy.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Why do we need a government? — Kautilya's 3 tasks

According to Kautilya, a king has 3 duties: raksha (protection from outside attack), palana (law and order), yogakshema (the welfare of the people). Today's governments do the same — peace, security, foreign relations, education, health, roads, the economy. A government has 3 functions: legislative (making rules), executive (implementing them), judicial (ensuring they are followed).

The school committee example

How should a School Student Committee be formed? (1) All the children as members — with such a crowd, work would come to a halt! (2) Let the Head Teacher choose — but not everyone's voice would be heard. (3) Choose representatives by vote — the best way, because everyone's voice is heard and work gets done too. This is democracy! Abraham Lincoln: democracy = 'government of the people, by the people, for the people'.

How do we tell governments apart?

Ask 4 questions: (1) Where does the authority come from — the people, or religion/lineage? (2) How is it formed — by election or by family? (3) Its parts and their work — does a constitution decide, or the king? (4) The goal — prosperity for all, or for a few families? The basic principles of democracy: equality, freedom, representative participation, vote for all, Fundamental Rights, an independent judiciary. India gave all adults the vote from 1950 itself — whereas in Switzerland women got it in 1971, in the UK full voting rights came in 1928, and in the USA only by 1965!

Direct, representative; parliamentary, presidential

Direct democracy — all citizens decide for themselves (in some parts of Switzerland); difficult in a big country. Representative — we elect representatives; elections are held every 5 years in India and every 4 years in the USA. Parliamentary (India, Australia) — the PM and ministers are part of the legislature; the confidence of the Lok Sabha is essential. Presidential (USA, South Korea) — the people directly elect the President; the confidence of the legislature is not needed. Legislature: India — Parliament (the Lok Sabha is more powerful + the Rajya Sabha); USA — Congress (Senate + House are equal); South Korea — a single house.

Our ancient republics and Uttaramerur

Vajji (Lichchhavi) — leaders chosen by merit and election → an early republic (where the head of state is elected, not hereditary). The Uttaramerur inscription (Chola, 10th century, Tamil Nadu) — the village assembly election: a sealed ballot box, the candidate's qualifications, duties, and immediate removal for corruption! In the republics of Greece and Rome, women, workers and slaves could not vote.

Even the king was bound by dharma

In India the king was bound by rajadharma — he did not have full freedom to do as he pleased. In the Shanti Parva of the Mahabharata, Bhishma taught rajadharma to Yudhishthira. In the Rajatarangini (Kalhana, 12th century), King Chandrapida wanted a cobbler's hut for a temple; the king asked for permission respectfully and paid compensation!

What other governments exist in today's world?

Absolute monarchy — the king holds all power (Saudi Arabia). Constitutional monarchy — the king is a figurehead, the real power lies with the PM and Parliament (UK, King Charles III). Theocracy — religious rules and religious leaders (Iran, Afghanistan, Vatican City). Dictatorship — all power in one person or group: Hitler (6 million Jews in the Holocaust), Idi Amin (Uganda — Indians had to flee); in North Korea the government decides even hair, clothes and the internet. Oligarchy — rule by a few rich and powerful people (oligos = few). Challenges to democracy: corruption, the gap between rich and poor, false information. More than half the countries in the world are democracies.

The Constitution of India — An Introduction

What the Constitution is, how it was made, what inspired it, and its main features — up to the Preamble.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

The Constitution — the country's 'rulebook'

To play Kabaddi you need rules — how many players, where the line is, how points are scored. In the same way, the rulebook for running a country is the Constitution: how the three organs of government are formed and what they do, the checks and balances between them, the rights and duties of citizens, and the country's long-term goals. It came into force on 26 January 1950 — that is why we celebrate Republic Day. The original copy is kept in Parliament in a glass case filled with helium (helium does not react with paper or ink).

Who made it, and when?

The Constituent Assembly met for the first time on 9 December 1946; it began with 389 members, which became 299 after Partition; among them were 15 women. The temporary chairman was Dr. Sachidananda Sinha, and then the president was Dr. Rajendra Prasad. The chairman of the Drafting Committee was Dr. B.R. Ambedkar — a social reformer and the first Law Minister. After about 3 years of hard work it was adopted on 26 November 1949 (Constitution Day) and came into force on 26 Jan 1950. Today it is the world's largest written constitution — 25 parts, 12 schedules (in 1950: 22 parts, 8 schedules).

Where did the inspiration come from?

(1) The freedom movement — equality, justice, freedom, fraternity, vote for all. (2) Our civilisation — acceptance of different views, respect for nature, vasudhaiva kutumbakam, sarve bhavantu sukhinah, janapada-sangha, rajadharma. (3) The world — 'a no bhadrah kratavo yantu vishwatah' (let good thoughts come from every direction): France → liberty, equality, fraternity; Ireland → Directive Principles; USA → an independent judiciary; the UK and Australia too. Prem Behari Narain Raizada wrote it by hand in beautiful calligraphy; Nandalal Bose's team made illustrations ranging from Mohenjo-daro, the gurukul, the Ramayana, the Gita and Nataraja to Nalanda!

Three key parts — Rights, DPSP, Duties

Fundamental Rights — you can get them enforced in court: Art. 14 (equality before the law), Art. 21 (life and liberty), protection against exploitation, Art. 21A (education). Directive Principles (DPSP) — guidelines for the government, not enforceable in court: Art. 38 (justice), 41 (welfare), 44 (Uniform Civil Code), 47 (nutrition, health), 48A (environment, wildlife), 49 (monuments). Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A, 1976) — respect for the Constitution, the flag and the national anthem; defending the country; the shared heritage; the environment; excellence; and (for parents) providing education to children aged 6–14.

A living document — it changes too

The Constitution is a 'living document' — it can be changed by an amendment after debate in Parliament. 1992 — the 73rd Amendment brought Panchayati Raj into the Constitution. 2004 — the Supreme Court held that flying the tricolour at home = freedom of expression! Begum Aizaz Rasul (1949): equality for women is not a new idea for India but an old ideal.

The Preamble — the Constitution's 'introduction'

'We, the People of India' — power belongs to the people. Sovereign (no outside power can give orders), Socialist, Secular (no official religion, equal respect for all religions), Democratic, Republic (the head of state is elected). Goals: Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity. 'Socialist', 'Secular', 'Integrity' were added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976. The President takes an oath to 'preserve, protect and defend' the Constitution.

From Barter to Money

From barter to coins, paper notes and UPI — the journey of money and its functions.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

When there was no money — barter

Imagine you have wheat and you need shoes. With no money, you would give wheat to the cobbler — this is the barter system: the exchange of goods/services without money. It was the world's first method of trade. Cowries, salt, tea, cloth, animals, seeds — all were used like 'money'. There were strange kinds of money too: the huge Rai stone wheels of Yap Island, the copper Tajadero of Mexico, the red-feathered Tevau of the Solomon Islands!

5 problems of barter — the farmer and the ox

A farmer wants to give his ox and get cloth, salt and utensils: (1) Double coincidence of wants — the cloth seller may not want an ox! (2) No single measure of value — one ox = how much cloth? (3) Divisibility — you cannot give half an ox! (4) Portability — it is hard to take an ox everywhere. (5) Durability — wheat rots. That is why money came into being.

Barter even today — Junbeel Mela

Junbeel Mela (Morigaon, Assam; jun = moon, beel = wetland) — 3 days, beginning with the Agni Puja; since the 15th century the Tiwa, Karbi, Khasi and Jaintia communities meet there and exchange hill produce for the plains' rice cakes — without money! Schools' book exchange clubs are barter too.

The 4 functions of money

(1) Medium of exchange — a means of buying and selling. (2) Store of value — earn today, spend later (it doesn't rot like wheat!). (3) Measure of value — the price of everything in one unit (rupees) — easy to compare. (4) Standard of deferred payment — borrow now, repay later.

The journey of coins — from 'rupa' to 'rupaya'

Cowrie → metal coins (~600 BCE) → paper notes (official 1861) → cards → UPI (2016). In ancient times kings issued coins (minting). Names: karshapana / pana; the mark punched on a coin = 'rupa' → and from this came the word 'rupaya'! 'Pana' survives even today — panam in Tamil/Telugu/Malayalam and hana in Kannada mean money. A coin has two sides: the obverse (head) and the reverse (tail). A Chalukya coin carries a Varaha, a Chola silver coin a tiger. In Pudukkottai (Tamil Nadu), Roman gold coins were found — trade favoured India! In 1947, 1 anna (1/16 of a rupee) bought a dozen bananas.

The ₹ symbol and paper notes

₹ — 2010; designer Udaya Kumar (IIT Bombay); the Devanagari 'Ra' + the Roman 'R'; the two lines at the top = the tricolour and the 'equals' sign. Paper money appeared first in China; in India in the late 18th century (Bank of Bengal, Bank of Bombay). Today only the RBI has the right to issue notes and coins — and printing them by anyone else is illegal. Notes carry heritage pictures and raised marks for visually impaired people.

Digital money and the Arthashastra's accounts

Today money isn't even visible (intangible) — debit/credit cards, net banking, UPI, QR code ('quick-response'); even Krishnappa's fruit cart has a QR! Arthashastra: an annual salary of 60 pana = one adhaka (~3 kg) of grain a day; failing to help a neighbour meant a fine of 100 pana — money also taught the values of society.

Understanding Markets

What a market is, how prices are set, types of markets, the supply chain, the role of the government and quality marks.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is a market — needs and wants

Market = a place where people buy and sell goods and services — a haat, a mandi, a marukatte (Kannada), a mall, or a phone app. Needs = essential for living (food, water, clothes, shelter); Wants = desires (a new video game) — not essential. Hampi Bazaar (Vijayanagara, 16th century, in front of the Virupaksha temple) — the Portuguese traveller Domingos Paes called it 'the best-provided city in the world'; Fernao Nuniz described its diamonds, pearls and cloth.

How is the price decided? — the guava story

A market needs: a buyer, a seller, and a price both accept, settled by haggling (bargaining). Guavas at ₹80 a kilo — too expensive, nobody will buy; ₹20 — a loss for the seller; ₹40 — 'just right'! Demand (how many people want to buy) and supply (how much stock there is) together decide the price. That is why vegetables get cheaper at night (stock is left, buyers are few) and woollens go on discount at the end of winter.

Types of markets

Physical (haat, local market, mall) vs online (apps, websites; online classes too). Domestic (within the country) vs international (export = selling abroad, import = buying from abroad). In 2024 India was the world's largest importer of vegetable oil (palm oil — Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand). India sells software to N. America, medicines to Africa, machinery to Europe, refined petroleum to West Asia; it buys diamonds from Africa, crude oil and fertilizer from West Asia, copper ore from S. America.

From farm to plate — the supply chain

Raw material → producer (the maker) → wholesaler (buys in bulk; godown, cold storage, mandi) → distributor → retailer (the neighbourhood shop) → consumer (you!). Online, an aggregator (app) delivers from the warehouse straight to your home. Wholesale markets: Khari Baoli (Old Delhi — spices, dry fruits), Bengaluru's flower market, Johari Bazaar (Jaipur — gems and jewellery). Surat — Asia's oldest textile market and the world's largest diamond industry (~15 lakh workers).

A market is not just trade, it is relationships too

Lifelong bonds with the tailor, the jeweller, the doctor; settling the grocer's account at the end of the month. Ima Keithal ('Mother's Market', Imphal, Manipur) — about 3,000 women run all the shops! In the south, shopkeepers give free haldi-kumkum — as a good wish.

What does the government do in the market?

(1) Price control — a ceiling on the price of life-saving medicines; a minimum support price for wheat, rice and maize (so farmers don't make a loss); minimum wages. (2) Quality and safety — testing of medicines. (3) Preventing harmful effects — a ban on single-use plastic, checking weights and measures. (4) Public goods — parks, roads, police. Kautilya too: a ghee seller must give 1/50 extra, because some sticks to the vessel — an ancient consumer protection!

Learn to read a packet — labels and quality marks

On a label: net quantity, manufacture date, batch no., best before, MRP, the maker's address, nutrition information, ingredients, allergens. Quality marks: FSSAI — food; ISI (BIS) — electrical goods, construction, tyres; AGMARK — farm produce (pulses, spices, honey); BEE Star — more stars = less electricity. People's opinions (word of mouth) and online reviews also influence purchases — but think carefully!

The Story of Indian Farming

The history of Indian farming, the link between weather, soil and water, Kharif-Rabi-Zaid, traditional and modern methods, and the challenges.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Farming — not just a plough and bullocks

Agriculture = Latin agri (field) + culture (cultivation): crop growing, animal rearing, forestry, gardening. Related activities: apiculture (honey bees), pisciculture (fish), sericulture (silk). About 18% of India's income (GDP) and about 46% of working people depend on farming; among those who farm in villages, 75%+ are women!

Farming thousands of years old

Rice grains in the Ganga plain date to the 7th–8th millennium BCE; at Mehrgarh (Baluchistan) barley and millets date to the 7th millennium BCE; Harappa had barley and wheat. The Vedas mention yava (barley), godhuma (wheat), vrihi (rice). Kalibangan (~2800 BCE) had criss-cross furrows — two crops grown together (intercropping) — and in the 1960s farmers there were still using the same method — 4,800 years later! Texts: Arthashastra, Krishi Parashara, Brihat Samhita (grafting), Vrikshayurveda. Xuanzang: 'climate and soil differ, so produce differs'. India has 15 agroclimatic zones.

Three crop seasons — Kharif, Rabi, Zaid

Kharif (monsoon, June–Sept) — rice, maize, jowar, bajra, sugarcane, groundnut, cotton. Rabi (winter) — wheat, barley, gram, peas, mustard. Zaid (summer) — watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, pumpkin. These are Arabic words used since the Mughal period; older names: kedara, haimana, graishmika. Arthashastra: a good monsoon = 1/3 of the rain at the start and end, 2/3 in the middle!

6 types of soil

Humus = dark organic soil formed from rotting plants and living things. (1) Alluvial — river deposits, the most fertile: rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute. (2) Black / cotton — from volcanic rock, holds moisture: cotton, jowar. (3) Red — red from iron, less fertile: ragi, groundnut. (4) Laterite — washed by rain: tea, coffee, rubber, cashew, coconut. (5) Desert/sandy — bajra. (6) Mountain — thin, stony: tea, spices, fruits. Protection: crop rotation, multiple crops, ploughing across the slope (contour ploughing), cow-dung manure, panchagavya (dung, urine, milk, curd, ghee); terracing, mulching, planting trees.

Managing water — old and new

Rain-fed (only on rainfall) vs irrigated (watered). Traditional structures: kul, kund, ahar-pyne, khadin, eri…; Munsar Lake (Gujarat, Queen Minaldevi, 11th century); Maharashtra's community Phad; the North-East's bamboo drip; Kallanai (~1,800 years old). Today: drip (drop by drop to the roots) and sprinkler (artificial rain) — both save water.

Seeds and old wisdom

Seeds were saved by families and communities for generations; Arthashastra — cotton seeds were coated with cow dung, and grains soaked in dew. ICAR's beejamrit. The old Indian seed drill — ploughing, sowing and closing the furrow all at once! Some company seeds cannot be used for the next crop — dependence grows. ICAR has recorded ~5,000 traditional methods; of 100+ tested, 85%+ are scientifically sound. Goa's Kulagar (family garden) and Gokrishi.

Green Revolution — gains and losses

1960s–70s: high-yielding (HYV) seeds, irrigation, chemical fertilisers and pesticides, machines → India became self-sufficient in food grains; its father figure M.S. Swaminathan (Bharat Ratna). But: weakened soil, falling groundwater, pollution, effects on health, harm to honey bees. Now: Sikkim — the world's first 100% organic state; neem pesticide; hydroponics (no soil, nutrients in water). FAO heritage sites (GIAHS): Kashmir's saffron, Odisha's forest farming, Kerala's Kuttanad (farming below sea level!).

Farmers' challenges and government help

The average farm is only ~3/4 of a hectare (about a football field; 1 hectare = 10,000 m²) — machines are costly; climate change; the debt trap. Pressure on the Ganga basin (50 crore+ people) — melting glaciers, heavy irrigation, groundwater overuse. Government: seeds and fertiliser, weather information, cheap electricity, PM Fasal Bima Yojana, government purchase of crops, cold storage, digital markets.

Tripartite Struggle (8th–9th century): the Palas (east), Gurjara-Pratiharas (west) and Rashtrakutas (Deccan) fought for Kannauj; no lasting winner. Palas: in 750 CE the people chose Gopala! Dharmapala built Vikramashila (Bihar) and Somapura; before entering Vikramashila, a dvarapandita ('scholar gatekeeper') tested you! Pratiharas: Nagabhata I — stopped the Arab invasions; Bhoja (9th century). Rashtrakutas: capital Manyakheta; Krishna I — Ellora's Kailashanatha temple (from a single rock!); Amoghavarsha I — ruled 64 years, a Kannada-Sanskrit poet.

Kashmir, Shankaracharya and the south

Kalhana's Rajatarangini ('river of kings', 12th century) — 'a historian should be free of love and hatred'. Lalitaditya defeated the Arabs; Queen Didda; Abhinavagupta. Adi Shankaracharya (8th century) — Advaita: Brahman alone is the ultimate truth; 4 maths at the four corners — Badrinath, Puri, Dwarka, Sringeri. Chalukyas: Pulakeshin II, capital Vatapi (Badami); Aihole has 100+ temples. Pallavas: Narasimhavarman I 'Mamalla' defeated Pulakeshin; Mamallapuram's Shore Temple and monolithic rathas. In the 9th century the Cholas rose again — Vijayalaya, Tanjavur; the Chola bronze Nataraja.

Rule, trade and society

Samantas (subordinate kings) supplied troops and local administration — but their loyalty was weak (the Rashtrakutas were once Chalukya samantas!). Divisions: bhukti/rashtra → mandala → village. Land grants increased. The Pallavas' tanks are still in use! From the western coast, trade with Iran, Iraq and East Africa; from the eastern coast, with South-East Asia and China. The Chera copper plates mention Christian, Muslim and Jewish traders. New castes formed; the system was flexible (the Palas were originally regarded as Shudras, later as Kshatriyas); but there was also discrimination such as 'untouchability'. Odisha's queen Tribhuvana Mahadevi.

The spark of Bhakti and the magic of mathematics

Bhakti began in the south: 12 Alvars (Vishnu; Andal a woman) and 63 Nayanars (Shiva; Karaikal Ammaiyar). Jain — Bahubali (Shravanabelagola, 10th century). Mathematics: Brahmagupta (598 CE, Bhillamala) — Brahmasphutasiddhanta: rules for zero, negative numbers and fractions — a founder of algebra! Bhaskara I — trigonometry; Virahanka — the same sequence later known as Fibonacci's; Mahavira (Jain) — the first maths text separate from astronomy. 'Monsoon' comes from the Arabic 'mawsim'; in 9th-century Baghdad, Sanskrit texts were translated → zero and the decimal system went from the Arabs to Europe — 'Hindu-Arabic numerals'.

The Hunas and the Arabs

The Hunas (Huns of Central Asia) reached the Ganga plain in the 6th century; Yashodharma (Mandsaur) defeated them; later they blended into society. Arabs: sea raids in 637 failed; in 712 Muhammad bin Qasim conquered Sindh and King Dahar was killed (Chachnama); but within two years Indian chiefs took back most of the territory; Nagabhata I and Lalitaditya stopped them — even 300 years later the Arabs held only two small kingdoms in Sindh-Punjab.

A difficult page of history — how to read it?

The impact of the Arab invasions was limited, but in the 11th–12th centuries Turkic powers pushed deep into north India. NCERT's lesson: understand the dark period too, but do not hold anyone today responsible for it. The Khyber Pass (Hindu Kush) has been a route for invaders and traders for 2,500 years; the Hindu Shahis guarded it.

Mahmud of Ghazni's 17 raids

Mahmud of Ghazni defeated the Hindu Shahi king Jayapala, then Anandapala in 1008; he took the title 'Sultan' (Arabic — power); 17 expeditions into India. 1018 — looted the grand temple at Mathura and Kannauj; 1026 — destroyed the Shiva temple at Somnath (Saurashtra). Today's Somnath temple was built in 1951 with public donations (inaugurated by Rajendra Prasad). Mahmud died in 1030; his nephew Salar Masud was stopped at Bahraich by King Suheldev. Plunder, killing, capturing and selling people — the aim was both loot and spreading religion.

Al-Biruni and Bhaskaracharya

Al-Biruni (from Khwarizm, in today's Uzbekistan) came with Mahmud, but learned Sanskrit and wrote an encyclopaedia on India; he gathered the knowledge of Aryabhata, Varahamihira and Brahmagupta, and translated the Yoga Sutras into Arabic; he wrote that science had 'fled' to places like Kashmir and Varanasi. Bhaskaracharya (Bhaskara II), born 1114 — Lilavati (maths through puzzles!), Bijaganita (algebra), Siddhanta Shiromani (astronomy).

Regional kingdoms of this age

Senas (Bengal) — the poet Jayadeva, Gitagovindam. Eastern Gangas — began the Puri Jagannath temple; later Konark. Kakatiyas — Warangal, stone gateways (torana), the Thousand Pillar Temple. Hoysalas — 'Poy, Sala!' (strike, Sala); the fine carving of Belur and Halebidu (UNESCO). Paramaras — King Bhoja (1010–55, Dhara): a huge lake → Bhopal ('Bhojpal'), the unfinished Bhojeshwar temple, the book Samarangana Sutradhara (architecture, machines) — different from the 9th-century Pratihara Bhoja!

The Cholas — lords of the sea

Rajaraja I (from 985) — the Maldives, northern Sri Lanka; Thanjavur's Brihadishvara (Rajarajeshvaram); allowed the Shrivijaya king to build a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam. Rajendra I (1014–44) — reached the Ganga → 'Gangaikondachola', new capital Gangaikondacholapuram; in 1025 a naval expedition against Shrivijaya (Malaysia-Indonesia) — for the Malacca trade, with no conquest. Sent envoys to China.

Ghuri, Prithviraj and Tarain

Muhammad Ghuri was defeated in 1178 near Mount Abu by the Chaulukya king Mularaja II (queen Naikidevi)! The Chauhan capital was Ajmer; Delhi = 'Dhillika' → Dhilli → Delhi. Prithviraj III defeated Ghuri in 1191 (Tarain I), but lost in 1192 (Tarain II). Ghuri kept the territory; his general Qutb-ud-din Aibak → later the Delhi Sultanate. Ghuri's coins carried Lakshmi!

The fire of Nalanda and new ideas

Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila; Nalanda's library burned for months → the decline of Buddhism quickened. Yet 30 years later, 90-year-old Rahula Shribhadra was still teaching 70 students — the flame of learning had not gone out! In 1205 Bakhtiyar was badly beaten in Kamarupa (Assam). Ideas: Ramanujacharya — Vishishtadvaita (liberation through bhakti and surrender); Basavanna — Lingayat, opposed caste discrimination, Kannada vachanas, the Anubhava Mandapa — a discussion hall for all. Khajuraho's Kandariya Mahadeva (Chandella). Cambodia's Angkor Wat — the world's largest religious monument, dedicated to Vishnu!

India, a Home to Many

The communities who came from outside and made India their home — Jewish, Syriac Christian, Parsi, Arab, Siddi, Armenian, Baha'i, the Polish children and the Tibetans.

'Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam' — the whole world is one family

Hitopadesha: 'This one is mine, that one is a stranger — this is the thinking of small minds; for the generous, the whole world is a family (vasudhaiva kutumbakam)'. Sri Sarada Devi: 'The whole world is your own'. For centuries India gave shelter to persecuted people. Swami Vivekananda (Chicago, 1893): 'I am proud to belong to a country which has sheltered the persecuted and the refugees of all religions and all nations'.

The Jews — Bene Israel and Kochi

Bene Israel — on the Konkan coast; according to some scholars they arrived around 175 BCE after a shipwreck; their scriptures were lost but they remembered the prayer to 'One God'; they are India's largest Jewish community. Later, Jews came to Kochi; the king gave them land — 'for as long as the world, the sun and the moon endure'; a synagogue was built.

Syriac Christians and the Parsis

Syriac Christians — considered 'heretics' in the Roman Empire and spies in Persia → they came to Malabar (Kerala). Parsis (Zoroastrians) — after the Arab conquest of Persia in the 7th century they faced forced conversion, the jizya tax, and the destruction of fire temples → they reached the Gujarat coast in the 8th–10th centuries. The famous story: Jadi Rana, the king of Sanjan, showed them a jug full of milk — 'there is no room here'. A Parsi elder stirred sugar into it — the milk did not spill! The meaning: 'we will mix in with you and add sweetness'. The sacred fire still burns at Udvada today. India has the largest number of Parsis in the world. The prophet is Zarathushtra, God is Ahura Mazda, the scripture is the Avesta; Vedic similarities: soma = haoma, yajna = yasna.

Arab traders, Siddis, Armenians, Baha'is

Arab traders came to Kerala and Gujarat from the 7th century — local marriages; the Mappila Muslims of Kerala; the Cheraman Juma Masjid — India's oldest mosque; they were peaceful traders, not conquerors. Siddis — of African origin, brought as slaves; today a Scheduled Tribe; Hirabai Lobi — Padma Shri 2023. Armenians — Akbar allowed a church in Agra; Abdul Hai was Chief Justice, Lady Juliana was the palace doctor; Armenian Street in Chennai. Baha'is — Baha'u'llah in Persia (1800s); the Lotus Temple in New Delhi.

The 'Good Maharaja' and Tibet

State and Government — know the difference

Democracy and Republic — they are not the same

Democracy = rule by the people. Republic = the head of state is elected, not hereditary. Legislature (makes laws — Parliament, Vidhan Sabha), Executive (implements them), Judiciary (upholds the law and protects the Constitution). A bank — the 'bridge' for money

A bank is like a bridge: on one side are savers (Navdeep deposited ₹3,000), on the other side are borrowers (Rima took a loan for her bamboo business). The bank accepts deposits and gives loans. Types of accounts: Savings (for ordinary people; earns interest; a limit on withdrawals), Current (for traders; no interest; no limit on transactions), Fixed Deposit (deposited once, for 3–5 years, higher interest). In the passbook, every transaction is recorded — Debit = money went out, Credit = money came in.

Interest on interest — the magic of compounding

Interest = the 'rent' for borrowing or lending money. Compounding = interest on the earlier interest too! ₹1,000 @ 6%: ₹1,060 in the 1st year, ₹1,123.60 in the 2nd year, and ₹2,012 in 12 years — almost double! The story of Ambalappuzha in Kerala: a sage asked for rice doubled on every square of the chessboard — 128 on the 8th square, 32,768 on the 16th, and by the 32nd more than 210 crore! This is called exponential growth.

How does a bank earn?

It pays low interest on deposits and charges high interest on loans. Anand deposited ₹200 @ 2% (got ₹4), Shreya took a ₹200 loan @ 5% (paid ₹10) → the bank earns ₹6. A bank does not lend out all the money — it keeps some as reserve. PM Jan Dhan Yojana (2014) — zero balance accounts; earlier there were only ~15 crore accounts, now 50 crore+ (mostly women); money goes directly into the account. Post office schemes: NSC, Kisan Vikas Patra, Sukanya Samriddhi; NABARD — loans for villages and farming.

The RBI — the bank of banks

Reserve Bank of India — set up in 1935, government-owned since 1949; the 'banker to banks' (central bank): prints and distributes currency notes, sets interest rates, lends to banks and the government. At the gate of the Delhi office stand a yaksha and yakshini — guardians of Kubera's treasure! In ancient India temples also acted like banks — lending to craftsmen and traders; records of loans on interest from the Kodumbalur (TN) temple.

Ways to send money, and UPI

Withdrawal slip, ATM (debit card + PIN, 24×7), cheque (date, name, amount in words and figures, signature), POS machine, net banking, mobile (BHIM). UPI — 2016, created by NPCI; instant payment by QR or phone number; it grew hugely during COVID; Nepal (2022) was the first foreign country, then the UAE, France, Sri Lanka, Bhutan and Mauritius — 'India's gift to the world'!

The share market and protection from fraud

A share = a part-ownership in a company — like one piece of a whole chapati! Shares are bought and sold on a stock exchange; the BSE (1875) is among the oldest exchanges in the world. Prices rise = boom, fall = crash — ups and downs come from a company's performance, government policy and war. Protect yourself from fraud: never share your OTP, PIN or password, and do not click unknown links. To complain: 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in.

Short notes for exams

  • Financial infrastructure = banks, payment systems, the stock market, financial institutions — the exchange and management of money.
  • Bank — accepts deposits from people and gives loans to borrowers. The story of Navdeep (deposited ₹3,000 in savings) and Rima (a loan for a bamboo business).
  • Types of accounts: Savings (individuals, earns interest, limit on withdrawals), Current (traders, no interest, no limit on transactions), Fixed Deposit (once, 3–5 years, higher interest than savings).
  • Interest = the rent for lending/borrowing money (%). Compounding = interest on the earlier interest too. ₹1,000 @ 6%: ₹1,060 in the 1st year, ₹1,123.60 in the 2nd year; in 12 years ₹2,012.20.
  • The story: the king and the sage of Ambalappuzha (Kerala) — rice doubled on every square of the chessboard: 128 on the 8th, 32,768 on the 16th, by the 32nd more than 210 crore — exponential growth!
  • Passbook — a record of all transactions. Debit = money went out, Credit = money came in.
  • How a bank earns: low interest on deposits, high interest on loans. Anand ₹200 @ 2% (got ₹4), Shreya loan ₹200 @ 5% (paid ₹10) → bank earns ₹6. A bank does not lend out all its deposits — it keeps a reserve.
  • PM Jan Dhan Yojana — 2014: zero balance accounts; earlier only ~15 crore accounts, now 50 crore+ (mostly women); direct transfer of wages/scholarships, fewer middlemen.
  • Post office: NSC, Kisan Vikas Patra, Sukanya Samriddhi. IFCI — industry (power, textiles); NABARD — rural development, agricultural loans.
  • RBI — set up in 1935; government-owned since 1949; 'banker to banks' (central bank): prints and distributes currency, sets the benchmark interest rate, lends to banks and the government. A yaksha and yakshini at the gate of the Delhi office (guardians of Kubera's treasure).
  • In ancient India temples were like banks — lending to craftsmen, traders and local governments; agreements on copper plates. Kodumbalur (TN, 13th c.) — loans on interest from the Tirumudukunramudaiya-Nayanar temple.
  • Withdrawing money: withdrawal slip, debit card + ATM (24×7) + PIN. Transfers: cheque (date, name, amount in words and figures, signature, MICR code), POS machine, net banking, mobile (BHIM).
  • UPI — 2016, NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India); instant by QR or phone number; popular during COVID; many languages. Nepal (2022) was the first foreign country; also the UAE, France, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Mauritius → 'India's gift to the world'.
  • Stock market: a share = part-ownership in a company (a piece of the chapati); buying and selling on a stock exchange. BSE — 1875, among the oldest exchanges in the world. Prices rise = boom, fall = crash; ups and downs due to company performance, government policy, taxes, war, economic shocks.
  • Protection from fraud: never share your OTP, PIN or password; do not click unknown links; do not store bank details on a device. Complaint: helpline 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in.

C. Short-answer (model answers)

  1. 1Why is India called a 'mini-continent'?

    Within a single country, India has the snow-clad Himalaya, the cold desert of Ladakh, the fertile Gangetic plains, the hot Thar desert, the ancient Aravalli, the Peninsular plateau, two long coastlines, deltas, coral and volcanic islands, and Meghalaya, which gets the highest rainfall in the world. Such geographical, climatic and biological diversity is usually found across a whole continent.

  2. 2How is the monsoon formed?

    In summer, land heats up faster than the sea, so low pressure forms over Asia. Wind blows from high pressure (the cool sea) to low pressure (the hot land) and brings moisture — this is the southwest monsoon (covering the whole country from June to mid-July). In winter it is the reverse: the land is cool and wind blows from land to sea — the northeast monsoon, which crosses the Bay of Bengal and brings rain to the south-east.

  3. 3Explain Kautilya's Saptanga.

    The seven limbs of the state: Swami (king), Amatya (ministers and officials), Janapada (territory and people), Durga (fortified cities), Kosha (treasury), Danda (army, law & order) and Mitra (allied states). Together the seven make a secure and prosperous state. Core principle: 'the king's happiness lies in the happiness of his people'.

  4. 4Why is the Gupta period called the 'classical age'?

    In a long period of peace and prosperity, science (Aryabhata — the Earth's rotation, length of the year; Varahamihira — Brihat Samhita), literature (Kalidasa — Meghadutam), medicine (the Charaka and Sushruta Samhitas given their final form), metallurgy (the rust-free Iron Pillar) and art (Ajanta, Sarnath, Deogarh) reached their peak. Old knowledge was compiled into texts that remained the standard for centuries.

  5. 5Difference between Fundamental Rights and DPSP.

    Fundamental Rights (such as Art. 14 equality, Art. 21 life and liberty, Art. 21-A education) are promises — one can go to court if they are violated. DPSP (Art. 38 justice, 44 UCC, 47 nutrition, 48-A environment) are guidelines for the government — they cannot be enforced through courts. The framers deliberately kept some rights for immediate effect and some goals to be achieved gradually.

  6. 6Difference between democracy and republic, with examples.

    Democracy = the people choose the government through free and fair elections. Republic = the head of state is elected (directly or indirectly), not a hereditary monarch, and the constitution limits power. India and the USA are both. The UK, Canada and Sweden are democracies but not republics, because a king/queen is the head of state there.

  7. 7Why is it important to balance traditional and modern methods in farming?

    The Green Revolution (HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, machines) brought self-sufficiency in grain, but it weakened the soil, lowered groundwater, and caused pollution and health problems. Traditional methods (crop rotation, panchagavya, phad, terrace farming) go along with nature but may give lower yields. ICAR found 85%+ of traditional methods to be scientifically sound — so a mix such as drip irrigation, neem pesticides and organic farming (Sikkim) is the best path.

  8. 8Explain the magic of compound interest with an example.

    In compound interest, you earn interest on the earlier interest as well. ₹1,000 @ 6% → interest of ₹60 in the 1st year (₹1,060), ₹63.60 in the 2nd year (₹1,123.60), and in 12 years ₹2,012.20 — nearly double. In the story of the king of Ambalappuzha and the chessboard, rice doubling on each square gave over 210 crore grains by the 32nd square. The longer the time, the faster the growth.
