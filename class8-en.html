Natural Resources and Their Use

When Nature becomes a 'resource', the types of resources, their uneven distribution, and how to use them wisely.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

First, a question — where did the button on your shirt come from?

Look around you — a pencil, a chair, clothes, electricity, even a plastic button. If we keep tracing back what each thing is made from, every single thing finally leads to Nature. A pencil comes from wood (a tree), plastic from petroleum (under the ground), cloth from cotton (a farm). So our whole life rests on the gifts of Nature.

When does a tree become a 'resource'?

A tree standing in a forest is part of Nature by itself — humans did not make it. When we cut its wood and make a chair, that same tree becomes a resource for us. But not everything becomes a resource. There are three conditions: (1) technology must be available — petrol hidden deep under the sea is useless until we have a machine to bring it out; (2) the cost must be reasonable — if getting it out costs so much that there is no benefit, it is useless; (3) society must accept it — for example, people do not cut the trees of a sacred grove (a holy forest).

Sorting resources into groups

Just as children in a school are divided by class, resources are divided into groups too. First way — by use: (a) essential for life — air, water, food (from soil); we cannot make these; (b) for making things — wood, marble, gold; (c) for energy — coal, petrol, gas, sunlight, wind, flowing water. Second way — whether they form again or not: renewable and non-renewable.

Renewable — they form again (but with a condition!)

Suppose you get a small cut on your finger — in a few days it heals by itself. Nature is like that too: a forest grows back after a fire. This is called restoration (returning to the earlier condition). Regeneration goes a step further — creating new life, like planting the area's native trees in a barren patch and seeing birds and squirrels all come back. In Nature there is no waste — a fallen tree rots into soil, and the soil grows new plants. Sunlight, wind, flowing water and forest wood are renewable. But there is a condition: if we cut trees so fast that the forest cannot grow back, even a renewable resource will run out. Take the tuna fish — it is declining because of overfishing.

Non-renewable — once gone, gone for good

Coal and petrol (fossil fuels), and minerals like iron, copper and gold, take millions of years to form. We use them much faster than they form. One estimate says India's coal will last about 50 more years. So we must spend them carefully (judiciously) — just like the money in a piggy bank.

What work does Nature do for us for free?

A forest cleans water, stops soil from being washed away and gives animals a home — these are ecosystem functions. When humans benefit from them (clean water, safe fields, pollination of flowers), they are called ecosystem services. An interesting fact: a big tree produces about 275 litres of oxygen a day, and one person needs about 350 litres every day!

Why are resources not equal everywhere?

Coal is in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, oil in Assam (Digboi) and Mumbai High, iron in Odisha and Karnataka — that is, resources are uneven. Where a resource exists, factories come up, jobs are created and cities grow. But often the people living there have to leave their homes. Rivers do not respect borders either — the water of the Kaveri has to be shared among Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Puducherry; the Brahmaputra flows through several countries.

'Paradox of Plenty' — can having a lot be a problem?

Imagine someone has plenty of cotton but doesn't know how to make cloth — they will sell cheap cotton and buy expensive cloth. In the same way, some countries are full of resources yet remain poor, because they could not build industries that make costlier goods from those resources. This is called the 'resource curse' / 'paradox of plenty'. India saved itself from this curse by building industries.

The story of Punjab — an emptying treasure of water

In the 1960s came the Green Revolution — new seeds of wheat and rice that gave higher yields but also drank more water. Farmers began pumping out groundwater; because of free electricity, even more was pumped. Add chemical fertilisers and pesticides. The result: about 80% of Punjab's area is 'over-exploited', in many places water is not found until 30 metres down, and chemicals have dissolved in the water. The country's stomach was filled, but a heavy long-term price is being paid.

The story of Sikkim — Pema's farm

Pema's family was in debt because of costly chemical fertilisers. When the government promoted organic farming, they started using compost, pest sprays made of neem and garlic, and mixed crops. At first the harvest was smaller, but in about 5 years the farm flourished. In 2016 Sikkim became a 100% organic state — birds and insects returned, tourists increased, and farmers' income rose by ~20%.

What can we do? — Be a 'steward'

A steward means a caretaker — one who does not own something but looks after it. Old methods (collecting rainwater, repairing ponds, cow-dung manure, mulching, multi-cropping), the knowledge of Vrikshayurveda, earthen buildings (Auroville), and solar energy (Bhadla Solar Park, International Solar Alliance 2015) — these are all ways forward. The Bhagavad Gita's lokasangraha says: rise above your own desires and work for the good of all.

Reshaping India's Political Map

13th–17th centuries: the Delhi Sultanate, Vijayanagara and the Mughals, and the Rajputs, Ahoms, Sikhs and Gonds who stood up to them — how the map kept changing.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What period does this chapter talk about?

Imagine a big map whose borders are rubbed out and redrawn every few years — that is the story of this chapter. The period is roughly the 11th to the 17th century — often called 'medieval' (the Middle Ages = 'between two ages'). This word was really coined for European history, so it does not fit India perfectly — for us it simply means the 11th–17th century. In this era, people from beyond the Hindu Kush mountains — Turkic and Afghan people from Central Asia — came again and again to invade, to gain wealth and land and to spread their religion.

The Delhi Sultanate — 5 dynasties, one after another

After Prithviraj Chauhan was defeated in 1192, the Delhi Sultanate was founded in 1206. 'Sultan' was a title. Five dynasties ruled: Mamluk (Slave), Khilji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi. There was a lot of bloodshed over the throne — about 2 out of every 3 sultans took the throne by removing the previous one, so a sultan's reign lasted only ~9 years on average.

Ala-ud-din Khilji and Muhammad bin Tughlaq

Ala-ud-din Khilji (early 14th century) led big campaigns in north and central India, and also stopped Mongol invasions. He had 'Sikander Sani' = the second Alexander written on his coins. His general Malik Kafur went as far as the south (Srirangam, Madurai, Chidambaram). Under Muhammad bin Tughlaq, almost the whole subcontinent came under one ruler for the first time since the Mauryas. But his plans backfired: (1) he moved the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (Devagiri) — people had to walk over 1,000 km, and then it was moved back to Delhi! (2) token currency — he gave copper coins the value of silver; the idea was ahead of its time (today's notes are 'tokens' too), but people started making fake coins and trade was disrupted.

Timur's invasion and the decline of the Sultanate

In 1398 the cruel Central Asian conqueror Timur came and looted and ruined Delhi. After that came the Lodi dynasty — the last dynasty of the Sultanate — but by then its territory had shrunk a lot. Some sultans imposed the jizya tax on non-Muslims. Temple idols were also broken — this is called iconoclasm.

Those who never gave up — resistance to the Sultanate

The Eastern Ganga king Narasimhadeva I (Kalinga/Odisha, 13th century) held back the Sultanate and, in memory of his victory, built the Sun Temple at Konark. The Musunuri Nayakas joined more than 75 chiefs together and drove the Tughlaq army out of Warangal (~1330–36) — imagine how hard it was to unite 75 leaders in those days! The Hoysalas (Karnataka; temples at Belur and Halebidu) remained the only independent kingdom in the south; the story of their name: Sala fought a lion and saved his guru — 'Hoy Sala!' (strike, Sala!). Rana Kumbha of Mewar built the fort of Kumbhalgarh, whose wall is 36 km long.

Vijayanagara — the hare that chased away the dogs!

In 1336 two brothers, Harihara and Bukka (earlier Tughlaq governors), broke away from Delhi and founded their own kingdom. The story goes that at Hampi they saw a hare turn around and chase hunting dogs! Their guru Vidyaranya said it was a sign of courage — build the capital here. To the north was the enemy Bahmani Sultanate (1347), which later broke into 5 Deccan Sultanates — Bijapur, Golconda, Berar, Ahmednagar, Bidar. Titles: the Vijayanagara king = Narapati, the Bahmani = Ashwapati, Odisha = Gajapati, the Marathas = Chhatrapati ('pati' = lord).

Krishnadevaraya and the destruction at Talikota

In the 16th century, under Krishnadevaraya, Vijayanagara was at its peak. He was a poet himself — he wrote 'Amuktamalyada' in Telugu (the story of the saint-poet Andal; it also has Rajaniti — ideas of good governance). He donated to Tirupati and the Vitthala temple — the 'musical pillars' of the Vitthala temple give out musical notes when tapped! The Portuguese traveller Domingo Paes wrote that the city was as large as Rome, 'the best-provided city in the world'. After Krishnadevaraya died in 1529, the Deccan Sultanates together defeated Ramaraya in the battle of Talikota in 1565; the city was plundered for months. Later the Nayakas began ruling small kingdoms of their own.

The Mughals — from Babur to Akbar

Babur (a descendant of Timur), driven out of Samarkand, defeated Ibrahim Lodi in the First Battle of Panipat in 1526 with gunpowder, cannon and guns — the Sultanate ended and the Mughals began. Babur's autobiography, the Baburnama, shows his love of birds and fruits as well as cruel massacres. His son Humayun was driven out by Sher Shah Suri, but Humayun came back. Himu (Hemchandra Vikramaditya) captured Delhi, but was wounded in the Second Battle of Panipat in 1556 and was beheaded on Akbar's orders.

Akbar — harsh at first, then tolerant

He became emperor at the age of 13. In his youth he besieged the fort of Chittor for over 5 months; after the victory he had about 30,000 people killed; the women there performed jauhar (leaping into fire to avoid being enslaved). Later Akbar changed his policy: marriages with Rajput princesses, a place for Rajputs in the court, abolition of jizya, and the idea of 'sulh-i-kul' (peace with all). He could not read or write himself, but at Fatehpur Sikri he set up a translation bureau — the Persian version of the Mahabharata, the 'Razmnama', the Ramayana (with 176 paintings), the Gita and the Panchatantra. His reign: 1556–1605 (~50 years).

Jahangir, Shah Jahan, Aurangzeb

Jahangir loved art; Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal. Aurangzeb killed his elder brother Dara Shikoh, imprisoned his father Shah Jahan in the Agra Fort and took the throne in 1658, taking the title 'Alamgir' (conqueror of the world). The Mughal empire became largest in his time, but the last 25 years were spent in wars in the Deccan and the treasury was drained. He brought back the jizya and a pilgrimage tax; in a 1669 order he commanded that temples and schools be demolished (Banaras, Mathura, Somnath). After his death in 1707, Mughal power declined fast. Remember: these are people of a bygone age — no one living today can be blamed for it.

Resistance to the Mughals — tales of courage

The revolts of Jat peasants (20,000 people). Tribal groups such as the Bhils, Gonds, Santhals, Koch. Rani Durgavati (Garha, a Gond kingdom) — 20,000 soldiers, 1,000 elephants; in 1564 she fought Akbar's general and, though wounded, did not surrender — she was only 40. Rana Sanga — lost to Babur at Khanwa. Maharana Pratap — Haldighati, 1576; he escaped and for years waged guerrilla warfare (surprise attacks from hiding) from the Aravallis; Bhil archers helped him, which is why Mewar's emblem includes a Bhil warrior. Durga Das Rathore protected the independence of Jodhpur.

The Ahoms and the Sikhs

The Ahoms came from Myanmar to the Brahmaputra valley in the 13th century. Paik system: every able-bodied man served the state through labour or in the army and got land in return — a big army without a standing army! Saraighat, 1671 (near Guwahati, on the Brahmaputra) — Lachit Borphukan's 10,000 soldiers defeated a Mughal army of 30,000. Guru Nanak (15th century) — Ik Onkar, equality, compassion. Guru Arjan compiled the Guru Granth Sahib; Jahangir had him tortured to death. Guru Hargobind raised an army. In 1675, Guru Tegh Bahadur refused to change his religion while protecting the Kashmiri Pandits; he was beheaded in Chandni Chowk (Gurudwara Sis Ganj Sahib). Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699. Maharaja Ranjit Singh founded the Sikh Empire in 1799.

How was the realm run and how did people live?

Sultanate: the iqta system — nobles (iqtadars) were given territory, collected taxes and maintained an army; the post was not hereditary. Akbar: ministers — Diwan (finance), Mir Bakhshi (army), Khan-i-Saman (public works, trade, royal household), Sadr (justice, religion, education); 12 subahs (provinces). Mansabdari: each officer (mansabdar) was given a rank (mansab) that fixed how many horses, elephants and soldiers he must maintain; salary was paid as land (jagir). Todar Mal surveyed the land and improved the revenue system. Farming was the main occupation — the land revenue was usually 1/5, sometimes up to 1/2. Irrigation with the Persian wheel. Mughal coins: the silver rupaya and the copper dam. Hundi = a written payment order, sending money without carrying it — the forerunner of today's banking. Temples were also centres of markets, loans and rest-houses.

The Rise of the Marathas

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj's Swarajya, the Maratha navy, the Peshwas, Tarabai and Ahilyabai, and Thanjavur — the largest Indian empire before the British.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Who were the Marathas?

The Marathas are the people of the Deccan plateau — especially present-day Maharashtra — who speak Marathi. Marathi literature has been written continuously since the 12th century. In the 13th century the Yadava dynasty ruled here (capital Devagiri), which the Khiljis defeated in the 14th century. Even amid political turmoil, the bhakti tradition continued — saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram and Ramdas explained the Gita and the Upanishads in Marathi. This gave society a strong cultural foundation, on which Maratha power later rose.

Childhood — the lessons of his mother Jijabai

Shivaji was born in 1630 in the Bhonsle family, to Shahji and Jijabai. His father was often away in the service of the Deccan sultans, so in the Pune jagir his mother Jijabai gave him good values and education. At that time the Deccan sultans kept fighting each other and ordinary people suffered. At just 16, Shivaji captured abandoned forts and strengthened them. Slowly his dream took shape — 'Swarajya' = our own rule, where people are safe and happy.

The navy — a remarkable step for its time

When the kingdom reached the western coast, Shivaji realised he needed a navy to protect the sea (1657). It was a revolutionary step — Bijapur had only merchant ships and the Mughal navy was weak too. Sea forts such as Sindhudurg were built. Once, in 1665, the ruler of Oman (Muscat) seized 4 of Shivaji's ships, but when he heard that Shivaji's navy had more than 100 ships, he got scared and hid in his harbour!

Afzal Khan and the 'waagh nakh'

Shivaji fought guerrilla warfare — small groups, great speed, surprise attacks, and full knowledge of the hills and forests. Bijapur sent the experienced general Afzal Khan. The two agreed to meet alone in the dense forest below Pratapgad fort. There Shivaji struck Afzal Khan down with the waagh nakh (a small weapon like a tiger's claws), and the Maratha soldiers hidden in the hills drove his army away.

Shaista Khan's fingers and Surat

The Mughal commander Shaista Khan kept attacking for 3 years. One night Shivaji slipped into his camp with only a few soldiers! Shaista Khan escaped, but a few of his fingers were cut off and he fled Maharashtra — it was like today's 'surgical strike'. In return, Shivaji plundered the Mughals' rich port Surat — about 1 crore rupees! But he did not touch religious places, and even spared the house of the charitable Mohandas Parekh. The news was printed even in London's newspaper, the 'London Gazette'.

Purandar, Agra and the baskets of sweets!

Aurangzeb sent the Rajput general Jai Singh. At Purandar Shivaji had to make a treaty — he had to give up a lot of territory. On Jai Singh's advice he went to the court at Agra (1666), where he was insulted by being made to stand with a defeated Mughal general. He left the court in anger, and Aurangzeb put him under house arrest. Shivaji played a trick — he started sending big baskets of fruit and sweets to holy men and nobles. The guards checked them for a few days, then stopped checking... and one day Shivaji and his son Sambhaji slipped out hidden in the baskets!

Coronation at Raigad and the southern conquest

In 1674, at Raigad fort, the coronation took place with Vedic rites; the title was 'Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati'; a new era began — the Rajyabhisheka shaka. Then came the dakshina digvijaya (southern campaign, 1677) — he won parts of present-day northern Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, which later became a 'safe land at the rear' against the Mughals. In the south he stopped the Dutch from trading in slaves — in those days Europeans captured and sold Indians, and no Indian power stopped them. He died of fever in 1680. Inspired by him, Chhatrasal built up Bundelkhand; the poet Bhushan wrote verses in his praise.

After Shivaji — Sambhaji, Rajaram, Tarabai, the Peshwas

His elder son Sambhaji became Chhatrapati; Aurangzeb conquered Bijapur and Golconda, captured Sambhaji, tortured and killed him, and took Raigad too. The younger son Rajaram went to Gingee (Tamil Nadu) — the war spread to the south. Aurangzeb could never leave the Deccan and died without defeating the Marathas. Then Tarabai (Rajaram's queen) sent big armies to the north — she was the 'architect' of the northward expansion. Slowly the Peshwa (prime minister) became the most powerful — Bajirao I and Nanasaheb spread Maratha rule across India: up to Lahore, Attock and Peshawar!

From Panipat 1761 to the British

In 1761, in the Third Battle of Panipat, they suffered a heavy defeat by the Afghans, but recovered quickly under Peshwa Madhavrao I. Mahadji Shinde (Scindia) took back Delhi in 1771. In the later part of the 18th century the Marathas were the British's biggest enemy. Three Anglo-Maratha wars (1775–1818) were fought — the Marathas won the first, but in the end, because of infighting and the British's better technology, Maratha power ended in 1818. That is why it is said — the British took India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals. Nana Phadnavis even joined Hyder Ali and the Nizam to form the first all-India anti-British alliance.

How did he rule? — 'don't even take a blade of grass'

Shivaji stopped hereditary posts and land grants; he paid every officer a salary from the treasury and transferred them often so that no one became too powerful. Widows of fallen soldiers got a pension, and their sons got jobs. He wrote to his officers: cut teak for the navy only with permission, but do not cut mango and jackfruit trees — people raise them like children. The Ashta Pradhan (8 ministers) assisted him. From other provinces he collected chauth (25%) and sardeshmukhi (+10%), and in return protected them. Justice was given by the panchayat, with very few death sentences; in towns there was a kotwal (police). The army: infantry, cavalry, navy; among cavalry the bargir (horse supplied by the state) and the shiledar (own horse). Rockets were used too!

Forts, Kanhoji Angre and the 'cartaz'

The finance minister Ramachandrapant Amatya wrote in the 'Adnyapatra' — 'forts are the heart of the kingdom'; even an enemy like Aurangzeb came, yet the kingdom survived because of its forts. In the 18th century Kanhoji Angre won sea battles. Europeans used to force Indian ships to buy a 'cartaz' (pass); the Marathas turned it around and began demanding passes from Europeans — annoyed, the Europeans called Kanhoji a 'pirate' (sea robber)! Shivaji's ships went as far as Mocha (Yemen), Muscat (Oman) and Malacca (Malaysia).

Culture, great women and Thanjavur

Shivaji's seal was in Sanskrit (instead of Persian) — 'growing like the new moon, for the welfare of the people'. The 'Rajya-Vyavahara-Kosha' replaced Persian words with Sanskrit/Marathi words. The saffron flag, rebuilding of temples. The Modi script for letters. Ahilyabai Holkar (Indore), even after losing her husband and son, ruled for 30 years; she had temples, ghats and wells built from Kedarnath to Rameswaram; rebuilt Kashi Vishwanath and Somnath; and promoted Maheshwar weaving. In Thanjavur Shivaji's half-brother Ekoji ruled; Serfoji II wrote the play 'Devendra Kuravanji'; Carnatic music and Bharatanatyam took shape; there was the Dhanwantari Mahal (free treatment), and the first printing press of any Indian king.

Theme B · Tapestry of the Past

The Colonial Era in India

The Portuguese, Dutch, French and British — how traders became rulers, how India's wealth was drained, and how people resisted (up to 1857).

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is colonialism? — a simple example

Imagine an outsider comes to open a shop in your neighbourhood. First he trades; then, slowly, by helping one side in quarrels, he becomes everyone's 'headman', takes the produce of your fields and sells you his own goods at high prices. This is colonialism: one country seizes another territory and imposes its own settlements, rule, economy and culture there. From the 15th century, Spain, Portugal, Britain, France and the Netherlands made colonies in Africa, Asia, America and even Australia. The reasons: rivalry with each other, new resources, markets and routes, plunder, spreading the Christian religion, and some scientific curiosity. They called themselves a 'civilising mission', but in reality freedom was snatched away and people were exploited.

Why was India so 'attractive'?

Even 2,000 years ago India traded with Greece and Rome — spices, cotton, ivory, gems, sandalwood, teak, wootz steel. According to the economist Angus Maddison, until the 16th century India accounted for at least 1/4 of the world's total income (GDP) — with China, one of the world's two largest economies. It was precisely such a rich country that became the target of greedy powers.

The Portuguese — trade, but also cruelty

In May 1498 Vasco da Gama reached Kappad (near Kozhikode/Calicut). On his second voyage he killed traders and bombarded Calicut from the sea. He took Goa in 1510. The cartaz system — ships without a Portuguese pass were seized. In 1560 the Inquisition (a religious court) began in Goa — Hindus, Muslims, Jews and 'wrong' Christians were persecuted, forced to convert, and temples were destroyed; it lasted until 1812. But Rani Abbakka I of Ullal (Karnataka) stopped the Portuguese by joining hands with neighbouring kings; Rani Abbakka II made fireballs from coconut shells and burned their ships! Her story is still sung in Yakshagana.

The Dutch and the French

The Dutch (17th century) — only trade, especially in spices; Surat, Bharuch, Kochi, Nagapattinam, Masulipatnam. In 1741, in the Battle of Colachel, Marthanda Varma, the king of Travancore, defeated the Dutch on both land and sea — a rare victory of an Asian power over Europeans! The French: Surat (1668), Pondicherry (1674). Governor Dupleix (1742–54) began two tricks that the British later adopted: (1) training Indians in European style as sepoys (soldiers), (2) installing puppet rulers and ruling from behind the curtain. After losing to the British in the Carnatic Wars (1746–63), the French were confined to Pondicherry alone. In 1748 Dupleix had Pondicherry's Vedapurishwaran temple demolished.

The British — how did traders become kings?

The English East India Company was given a charter by Queen Elizabeth I — even with the freedom to keep its own army! In the 17th century, posing as 'only traders', they set up trading posts at Surat, Madras, Bombay and Calcutta. Then came the trick of 'divide and rule' — in the quarrels among kings and disputes over the throne, giving an army to one side to become the 'kingmaker'; and stoking tension between religious communities.

Plassey 1757 — 'Mir Jafar' = traitor

When a quarrel arose with Siraj-ud-daulah, the Nawab of Bengal, Robert Clive conspired with the Nawab's commander Mir Jafar — 'you betray him, we will make you Nawab'. At Palashi (Plassey) (~150 km from Kolkata), Mir Jafar's army — most of the Nawab's army — stood watching, and the small British force won. Even today 'Mir Jafar' means traitor!

The Doctrine of Lapse and the Subsidiary Alliance — two cunning traps

Doctrine of Lapse: if a king had no natural-born son, his kingdom went to the British — an adopted son was not accepted, though adoption was lawful in Hindu tradition. Subsidiary Alliance: a British 'Resident' would sit in the king's court, the king would maintain a British army at his own expense, and would deal with foreign powers only through the British — that is, the king paid the bill for his own slavery! Hyderabad (1798) was the first. It was called 'empire on the cheap'. At the time of independence there were 500+ princely states, about 40% of the subcontinent.

From 'heaven' to 'hell' — the terrible famines

Clive called Bengal the 'paradise of the earth'. Then the Company got the right to collect land revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. Two years of crop failure, plus forced cash taxes — the Bengal famine of 1770–72: about 1/3 of the population, ~1 crore people died, and the Company raised the tax! People were forced to sell seeds, bullocks, tools and even children, and to eat tree leaves. The Great Famine of 1876–78 — ~80 lakh died (mostly in the Deccan), yet about 10 lakh tonnes of rice were sent to Britain every year. Viceroy Lytton said the government would not interfere to lower food prices, and that same year a week-long feast for 68,000 people was held in Delhi! In total, 5–10 crore people died of famine under British rule — about as many as in World War II.

The 'drain' of wealth — money from India to Britain

The US historian Brooks Adams said that after Plassey the loot of Bengal reached London and Britain's Industrial Revolution began from 1760. Dadabhai Naoroji (the first Indian elected to the British House of Commons) wrote 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' in 1901; R.C. Dutt wrote 'Economic History of India'. According to Utsa Patnaik, about 45 trillion US dollars was taken out between 1765 and 1938 — about 13 times Britain's GDP in 2023! Railways, the telegraph, even the cost of Britain's wars were recovered from Indians — 'Indians funded their own subjugation'.

Cloth, villages and schools — everything changed

Industry ruined: Indian cloth faced heavy taxes in Britain, while British goods entered India almost tax-free — weavers became poor. William Bentinck (1834): 'the bones of the cotton-weavers are bleaching the plains of India'. By independence India's share of world GDP had fallen to ~5%. Village panchayats: Charles Metcalfe said villages were 'little republics' that survived the fall of every dynasty; the British broke them up and created a tax-collecting bureaucracy; courts were expensive, slow and in a foreign language. Education: by 1830 Bengal and Bihar had 1–1.5 lakh village schools. In 1835 came Macaulay's 'Minute' — 'a single shelf of a good European library is worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia' (though he knew neither Sanskrit nor Arabic!). The aim: a class of people 'Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste' — a cheap clerical class.

The railway — a boon or a trick?

The railway connected people, but its real purpose was to carry raw material from the interior to the ports and to spread British goods across the country, and also to rush troops quickly to places of revolt. And its cost was met from Indian taxes. The British called India the 'jewel in the crown' and claimed that in their empire 'the sun never sets'.

Resistance — sannyasis, tribals, peasants

The Sannyasi-Fakir Rebellion (Bengal, after the famine of 1770) — on it Bankim Chandra wrote the novel 'Anandamath' (1882), which contains 'Vande Mataram' — today our national song. Tribals had their forests taken away, were subjected to cash taxes and debt traps, and to the law on 'criminal tribes'. Kol Uprising (1831–32), Chota Nagpur — Munda, Oraon. Khasi Uprising (1829–33), Meghalaya. Santhal Rebellion (1855–56) — the brothers Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu, 'till the last drop of blood'. Indigo Revolt (1859–62) — peasants in Bengal were forced to grow indigo; with the support of Bengali newspapers some of the cruelty was stopped.

1857 — the Great Rebellion

The British called it the 'Sepoy Mutiny'; after independence historians called it the 'Great Rebellion of 1857'. Earlier, at Vellore in 1806, sepoys were enraged by a ban on the tilak and beards. In 1857 came the rumour — cartridges greased with cow and pig fat. At Barrackpore, Mangal Pandey attacked officers; his hanging spread anger. The sepoys of Meerut marched to Delhi and declared the aged Mughal Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader. It spread to Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi. Nana Saheb (Kanpur), Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi; captured Gwalior with Tatia Tope; martyred on 18 June 1858 — even a British officer called her 'the best and bravest'), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Awadh/Lucknow; took refuge in Nepal). The British revenge was extremely cruel. The rebellion lost for lack of a central leadership, but it sowed the seed of freedom. In 1858 — the Company's rule ended and the British Crown ruled directly (the British Raj); Queen Victoria's proclamation was issued.

Some unintended effects

The British carried out surveys, studied monuments and began archaeology — but they also took thousands of sculptures, paintings, jewels and manuscripts to Europe; efforts are now under way to bring them back (repatriation). In 1785 Charles Wilkins made the first English translation of the Bhagavad Gita. Hegel called the discovery of Sanskrit 'the discovery of a new continent'. The lesson: politics flows one way, but culture can flow in the opposite direction too.

Universal Franchise and India's Electoral System

Every citizen aged 18+ gets one vote, one value — how the Election Commission conducts elections for the Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, President and Vice President.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

'It's just voting!' — so why all the excitement?

An election is called the 'Festival of Democracy' because on this day every adult in the country — rich or poor, educated or not — becomes equal. Everyone's vote counts the same. Franchise / suffrage = the right to vote. Universal adult franchise = every citizen aged 18 years or above has the right to vote, regardless of caste, religion, gender, education or income. This is in Article 326. The age used to be 21; in 1988 it was lowered to 18. No one can vote in someone else's place, and to vote you must have your name on the voter list of your constituency (electoral area).

Why were our Constitution makers so bold?

In 1947 only about 14% of people were literate (women about 8%). Some people said that only the educated should be allowed to vote. But the Constitution gave the vote to everyone from day one — complete trust in the common person! Before independence only about 13% of Indians could vote. In many parts of the world, women had to fight long battles for the vote (a parade in the USA in 1913); in Switzerland women got the vote in 1971, while in India they had it from the very start. An inscription at Uttaramerur from the 10th century also describes a transparent election method — names were written on palm leaves and put in a pot, and a small child drew out a slip!

How big is our election?

2024 Lok Sabha: more than 96.8 crore voters, 543 seats, over 10 lakh polling stations. India's voters number 15 times the entire population of the UK! The country has 31 lakh elected representatives (13 lakh of them women) and 2.5 lakh+ local bodies. Even so, in 2024 about 34% of people did not vote — think about why?

No voter left behind

The Election Commission reaches remote areas — a polling station was set up even for a single woman voter! In 2024, for the first time, elderly and differently abled people could vote from home; some people could use a postal vote; there are braille voter cards, and an app for wheelchair and ramp facilities. Shyam Saran Negi (a teacher from Himachal) was the first voter of the first general election in 1951; he voted again in 2017 at the age of 100!

A class election — understand the whole system

The Class Representative election in Class 8 of Suryodaya School: Ahmed (cleanliness), Gurmat (peer coaching), Ravi (arts). Teacher Ms Usha = Election Officer. Secret ballot — no one knows who voted for whom. A braille ballot for Neha. 33 votes — 1 blank (invalid); Ahmed 8, Gurmat 12, Ravi 10. Gurmat won — even though she got less than half the votes! This is 'First-Past-the-Post' — whoever gets the most votes wins. What if someone likes none of the candidates? NOTA (None Of The Above) — it doesn't change the result, but it sends the message 'we need better candidates'.

Election Commission of India (ECI) — the referee of elections

The ECI is an independent constitutional body, set up in 1950. It conducts the elections for the Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, Vidhan Sabha, President and Vice President. The first general election was in 1951–52. Its tasks: fixing dates, registering political parties, enforcing the Model Code of Conduct, and overseeing the whole process. Structure: Chief Election Commissioner + 2 Election Commissioners (national); Chief Electoral Officer (state); District Election Officer, Returning Officer (conducts the election), Electoral Registration Officer (voter list). Panchayat and municipal corporation elections are conducted by the State Election Commission.

What happens inside the polling booth?

1st polling officer: checks your name on the list and your ID. 2nd: puts ink on your finger, gives a slip, takes a signature. 3rd: takes the slip and checks your finger. Then press the button on the EVM (Electronic Voting Machine) — beep! Also check the slip printed in the VVPAT to be sure your vote went to the right place. India's EVM-VVPAT has also been used in Namibia and Bhutan.

Model Code of Conduct — the 'rulebook' of elections

During elections: the government cannot announce new schemes that might tempt voters; government officials cannot campaign for a party; candidates must stay peaceful and not abuse anyone; giving saris, mixers or cash in exchange for votes is punishable. This code started in Kerala in 1960 with the agreement of the parties, was extended by the ECI in 1962, and has been enforced strictly since 1991. T.N. Seshan (CEC, 1990) made elections fearless and clean by introducing the voter ID, rules for campaigning and monitoring of spending.

Direct and indirect elections — MP, MLA, PM, CM

Direct: we vote directly — the MP of the Lok Sabha, the MLA of the Vidhan Sabha. In the Lok Sabha, 84 seats are reserved for SC and 47 for ST. The leader of the party or coalition (alliance) that wins a majority in the Lok Sabha becomes Prime Minister; in a state, Chief Minister. Indirect: elected people elect others — like class reps together choosing a school council. Rajya Sabha: of 245 members, 233 are elected by MLAs and 12 are nominated by the President; seats depend on population (more for UP, fewer for Arunachal); it is never dissolved (a Permanent House); the term is 6 years, and 1/3 of members retire every 2 years; elected by single transferable vote.

How are the President and Vice President elected?

President: an Electoral College — the elected MPs of both Houses + the elected MLAs of all states and of Delhi and Puducherry; single transferable vote. Nominated members and members of the Legislative Council do not vote — so that only people directly elected by the public choose the President. Vice President: all members of both Houses, elected and nominated; the Vice President is the Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha and carries out the President's duties when needed.

Challenges — and our role

The growing influence of money in elections, candidates with criminal records, and voter apathy (lack of interest in voting) in cities. The remedy: alert voters. Ask the right questions, vote thoughtfully — an alert voter is democracy's greatest protector.

Theme D · Governance and Democracy

The Parliamentary System: Legislature and Executive

The structure of Parliament, the journey from Bill to law, the accountability of the PM and Council of Ministers, and the Centre-State framework.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

The new Parliament building — starting with a school trip

Students of Nalanda Vidyalaya visited the new Parliament building in Delhi. It is neither round nor square — it is triangular, a sign of balance and of taking everyone along. Thanks to green construction, it uses about 30% less electricity. The walls show peacocks, lotuses, tribal art, temple designs and freedom fighters; the Ashokan emblem is there too. The old building (built in the 1920s) is now called 'Samvidhan Sadan' (Constitution House) — the Constituent Assembly met there.

What is Parliament made of?

Parliament = President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha. Having two Houses is called bicameral (bi = two, cameral = chamber/House). The Lok Sabha (House of the People / Lower House) — elected directly by the people; the Constitution provides for a maximum of 550 members. The Rajya Sabha (Council of States / Upper House) — indirectly elected. In both, seats are allotted according to each state's population. There have been 17 Lok Sabhas since 1952; the 18th Lok Sabha was formed in June 2024. Why two Houses? Because India is federal — power is shared among the Centre, the States and local government, so a separate House is needed to give the states a voice.

Where did our parliamentary system come from?

It took inspiration from the British system, but it also drew on our own experience: limited participation under British rule, the republics of the ancient Mahajanapadas, and the village panchayat, where elders took decisions together.

Who runs the House? — the Speaker and the Sengol

Each House has a presiding officer. The members of the Lok Sabha elect the Speaker, who gives members permission to speak, keeps order and enforces the rules. The Rajya Sabha is presided over by the Vice President. Near the Speaker's chair stands the Sengol — a silver sceptre with a gold coating, which was given to Pt. Nehru on 14 August 1947 as a symbol of the transfer of power. It comes from the Chola tradition — a reminder that power should follow dharma (righteousness); the Nandi on top is a symbol of justice. Parliament earlier had interpretation in 12 languages; now Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Manipuri, Urdu and Sanskrit are added too — 18 in all.

Legislature and Executive — making laws vs implementing them

Think of a school where a committee makes the rules (legislature) and the principal and teachers carry them out (executive). The Union Executive = the President, the Vice President, and the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Ministers are chosen from among the MPs of both Houses and are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Parliament has 4 functions: (1) constitutional — electing the President/VP, amending the Constitution, separation of powers, federalism, protecting Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; (2) lawmaking; (3) holding the executive accountable; (4) financial accountability.

From Bill to Act — the RTE in its own words

A Bill = the draft of a proposed law; once passed, it becomes an Act. The RTE says: my roots were in the Directive Principles, which were to be implemented within 10 years, but that did not happen. In the 1990s it was argued in court that education is part of the Right to Life. The 86th Amendment, 2002 added Article 21A — free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14. The Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha, examined by a committee, and the biggest question was — money! In August 2009 the Lok Sabha passed it, the President gave assent — and it became the RTE Act 2009. The path: introduction and reading in either House → (sometimes) Standing Committee → clause-by-clause discussion and amendments → vote → the same in the other House → Presidential assent → notification in the Gazette. A Money Bill (tax, borrowing) can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and only on the President's prior recommendation.

How is the government questioned?

Question Hour — usually the first hour of a sitting, when MPs ask ministers questions and the minister has to answer. Standing committees (MPs from different parties) ask ministries for reports — for example, the Health committee made recommendations about the Ayush department, and the ministry replied that 24 states have a directorate. Parliament passes the budget and checks where public money was spent. Sometimes there is poetry and humour in the House too — Nirmala Sitharaman quoted a couplet from the Tirukkural ('as living beings look to the rain, the people look to good governance'); and there was the poetic exchange between Sushma Swaraj and Dr. Manmohan Singh (2011).

The President, the PM and 'moral responsibility'

The President = Head of State, the nominal head of the executive: appoints the PM and ministers, summons Parliament, gives assent to Bills. The Council of Ministers advises the President; but when no party has a clear majority, the President can decide using his or her own discretion. The Prime Minister = the real (de facto) executive head — the leader of the majority party/alliance in the Lok Sabha; leads the Council of Ministers, coordinates the ministries, advises the President and sets national policies. Most Bills are brought by the government. Civil servants (officials) implement policies. In 1956 Railway Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri resigned, taking moral responsibility for a train accident — Nehru refused to accept it, but he would not change his mind.

The third pillar — the Judiciary and 'checks and balances'

The Judiciary (courts) interprets laws, settles disputes and protects Fundamental Rights — it is the guardian of the Constitution. If a law made by Parliament or an action of the executive goes against the Constitution, the court can stop it. The three organs — legislature, executive, judiciary — keep a watch on one another so that no single one becomes too powerful; this is called separation of powers and checks and balances.

A similar structure in the states

In a state, the Governor (appointed by the President) is the nominal head and the Chief Minister is the real one. The CM is the leader of the majority in the Vidhan Sabha; the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Vidhan Sabha. The term is 5 years. A state legislature is either unicameral (only the Vidhan Sabha) or bicameral (Vidhan Sabha + Vidhan Parishad) — only 6 states are bicameral: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh. Subjects are divided into three lists: the Union List (Centre only), the State List (state only), the Concurrent List (both; if there is a clash, the Centre's law prevails — like education, which is why the RTE applies across the country). Karnataka's Vidhana Soudha — the dream of Kengal Hanumanthaiah.

Challenges — why is Parliament functioning less?

Parliament has 3 sessions a year: Budget, Monsoon, Winter; about 6 hours a day. But the number of sittings is falling: the 1st Lok Sabha had 677 sittings, the 13th only 356. M. Venkaiah Naidu (2021): Rajya Sabha productivity was about 78% in 2004–14, then about 65%; the lowest was 35.75% in 2018. Reasons: absenteeism, disruptions, obstruction of Question Hour, members with criminal records. The remedy: alert citizens. Atal Bihari Vajpayee: 'Governments will come and go... but this country must endure, and the democracy of this country must live forever.'

Theme E · Economic Life Around Us

Factors of Production

The 4 inputs for making anything — Land, Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship — and Technology, which makes them more powerful.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Ratna's eatery — the whole story of production

Your clothes, shoes, bag and phone all reached you after passing through a production process. The things (inputs) used to make goods or services are called factors of production. Example: Ratna's small highway restaurant, 'Pause Point'. To start it she needed: a place (land), people to cook and serve (labour — now 7 people), money for utensils, furniture, gas and rent (capital), and the mind and courage to put it all together — Ratna herself (the entrepreneur). A shop, factory or firm that works like this is called a business / enterprise.

1. Land — not just soil!

In economics, 'land' means more than a piece of ground — soil, forests, water, air, sunlight, minerals, oil, gas — all natural resources. (Remember Chapter 1!) A business either buys land or takes it on rent.

2. Labour and Human Capital — effort vs skill

Labour = the physical and mental effort that goes into production — farmers, workers, carpenters, teachers, doctors. Human capital = the quality of that effort — special skill, knowledge, ability, experience. Two people may put in the same effort, but the one with better skill will work better and faster. Three facilitators that build human capital: (a) education and training — a civil engineering student studies design, then learns through training on site; (b) health — a healthy child attends school every day and learns better (cognitive development), and a healthy worker can do more work; (c) the social-cultural environment — Japan's kaizen ('continuous improvement', since the 1940s), Germany's work ethic (punctuality, attention to detail, quality).

India's strengths and challenges

Adult literacy (age 15+, able to read and write a short everyday statement) — in 2023 men ~85%, women ~70% (World Bank). Economic Survey 2024: about 65% of Indians are under 35 years of age! This can bring a demographic dividend — when there are many workers and few dependants, a country grows fast. But there is a condition: good education, health and training. Think about Harsh, who left school when his father lost his job — how will that affect his skills as an adult? And remember — every job is important: what would happen without sanitation workers, farmers or doctors?

Our old tradition of 'skill'

In ancient India work was a sadhana (devoted practice) — done with full dedication, like an offering to the deity or the customer. Tools were worshipped — even today there is Vishwakarma puja / Ayudha puja. Work = art (kala) + knowledge (vidya), passed down generation to generation. The Shilpa Shastra even recorded the measurements, poses and colours for sculpture, painting, buildings, woodwork and jewellery. The craftsman families who built temples often did not live to see the finished temple, yet worked for excellence. Stitched shipbuilding — a technique more than 2,000 years old: wooden planks were stitched together with rope instead of nails to build ships, whose flexibility let them sail comfortably in the Indian Ocean. It declined after the Europeans arrived in the 16th century; today it survives only in small fishing boats.

3. Capital — money and machines

Capital = money + durable human-made things used in production: machines, tools, vehicles, carts, computers, shops, factories. Where does the money come from? First your own savings, family, friends. If that falls short, a bank loan — Ratna took one too and paid interest along with the instalments. Big companies raise money from the public by selling shares on the stock market, and pay a dividend out of their profits.

4. Entrepreneurship — dream + risk

An entrepreneur is someone who: identifies a problem and sticks with finding a new solution, invests money and time and takes a risk, combines the other three factors, makes the necessary decisions, and does good for society with a new idea. An entrepreneur also creates jobs. A startup = a new venture that grows fast with few resources, using technology. Example: J.R.D. Tata (born 1904) — started India's first airline, Tata Airlines, in 1932 (later Air India); also steel, cars, electricity, chemicals; he cared for his workers; Bharat Ratna 1992.

Technology — the enabler

Technology = the use of scientific knowledge. It lets us make more goods with fewer inputs. UPI for payment in one click, early weather warnings, GPS for a shorter route, drones to spray fertiliser, robots in surgery. Email instead of letters. But not every old thing disappears — the pulley and wheelbarrow are still useful today. To learn online there is SWAYAM (from Class 9, free MOOCs — robotics, aquaculture, textile printing), and to find jobs the National Career Service portal.

How are all the factors connected?

They work together like pieces of a puzzle; if even one is missing, work stops. Farming, construction, handicrafts need more workers (labour-intensive); semiconductor chips and satellites need more machines and money (capital-intensive). New technology changes the proportions — machines mean fewer workers in farming; 3-D printing allows large-scale production of dying handicrafts. A supply chain = the whole network from production to sale; dependence on distant inputs brought production to a halt during COVID-19. In 2025 India became the world's second-largest mobile phone manufacturer (after China) — R&D → land/factory → raw materials → assembly + software → testing → mass production → shops.

Responsibility — towards both nature and workers

Tamil Nadu's leather factories provide jobs, but their waste pollutes rivers and soil; if old phones are not recycled, lead and mercury get into the ground and water. So producers should use resources carefully, cut waste and prevent pollution. For workers: fair wages and a safe environment, skill training, rights and protection (no discrimination, health care, leave). CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) — in 2014 India became the first country in the world to make a law requiring companies to spend 2% of their average profit of the previous 3 years on social welfare.

World Geography: Some Glimpses

A tour of the world — map projections, oceans, reefs, and the special landforms of each continent and the lives linked to them.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Earth — Bhudevi, Vasudha

Tribal communities and Indian tradition regard the Earth as a mother — Bhudevi or Vasudha. Our lives are connected to faraway things: even the far southern Indian Ocean affects the monsoon of the Ganga plains! Let's take a quick tour of the 'blue planet'.

Why does a map never show the whole truth? — the orange peel

The Earth is not perfectly round — it is an oblate spheroid: slightly bulging at the equator and slightly flattened at the poles (because of its spin). Take an orange, peel it and try to lay the peel flat on the ground without tearing it — impossible! In the same way, showing a round Earth on flat paper (map projection) always involves some distortion. The Mercator projection — shape and direction are correct, so it is useful for navigating ships; but near the poles sizes become very large — Greenland looks as big as Africa and bigger than India, though in reality it is smaller than India! The Robinson projection — a balance between size and shape. So look at several projections. Professionals use accurate maps with latitude and longitude. The language of a physical map: blue = water, green = plains, yellow/brown = plateaus, dark brown = high mountains, light yellow = desert, white = snow.

Oceans — the blue of the Earth

Oceans (salt water) cover ~71% of the Earth's surface and hold 97% of its water. They balance the climate — they absorb heat and release it slowly, and help in cloud formation and rain. Nearly half of our oxygen is made by the tiny plankton of the oceans (they make food from chlorophyll + sunlight + CO₂). The largest creature, the blue whale — over 30 m long, ~1,50,000 kg! The five oceans: Pacific (largest and deepest), Atlantic (a busy trade route), Indian (warm water, trade), Arctic (smallest and shallowest, polar bears, walruses), Southern (surrounds Antarctica, penguins and seals). The ocean floor too has mountains, valleys and plains!

The ocean's 'rivers', deep trenches and reefs

Ocean currents are like giant rivers flowing in the sea — warm (from the equator towards the poles) and cold (from the poles towards the equator). The Gulf Stream carries warm water to Western Europe, which is why the UK is warmer than other places on the same latitude. The Mariana Trench is the deepest trench; its Challenger Deep is more than 11,000 m deep — 2,000 m deeper than Everest is high! It is dark there, so creatures get energy from chemicals instead of sunlight. The Great Barrier Reef (off the north-east coast of Australia) — 2,000 km long, ~3,50,000 sq km, a UNESCO site; reefs are the 'rainforests of the sea' — home to about 1/4 of marine life. Pollution, overfishing and climate change cause coral bleaching.

Sea, Gulf, Bay, Strait, Canal — know the difference

A Sea — a large, partly enclosed body of salt water (Arabian Sea). A Gulf — a large part of the sea that cuts deep into the land (the Persian Gulf — for thousands of years the route of India-Europe trade). A Bay — a smaller, wide inlet; safe for ports (the Bay of Bengal — the largest bay in the world). A Strait — a narrow passage joining two seas (the Strait of Malacca). A Canal — a man-made waterway (the Suez Canal, 1869 — joins the Red Sea to the Mediterranean; the shortest sea route from India to Europe).

Asia — Tibet, Gobi, Steppes, Urals

The Tibetan Plateau — the world's highest and largest plateau, the 'Roof of the World'; formed by the collision of the Indo-Australian and Eurasian tectonic plates (the Himalaya too); ~2,500 km × 1,000 km, average height 4,500 m+; the source of the Indus, Brahmaputra and Yangtze; after the poles it holds the most fresh water — the 'Third Pole'. A desert = less than 25 cm of rain a year; about 1/5 of the land; ~1 billion people live there. The Gobi (Mongolia + northern China) — Mongolia is the 'Land of the Eternal Blue Sky' (8–9 months of sunshine), winters of −30°C; solar and wind energy. The Eurasian Steppes — a grassland 8,000 km long, a carbon sink; a resting place for the Siberian crane; the yurt (nomads' tent); the steppe tortoise was among the first living beings to travel around the Moon in 1968! The Urals — old mountains, 2,500 km, the boundary of Europe and Asia; iron, copper, platinum, gold, oil, gas; taiga (coniferous forest) and tundra (frozen soil — permafrost; if it melts it releases greenhouse gases).

Mountains = 'water towers'

Mountains are formed by tectonic forces, volcanoes and erosion. They shape the climate, are hotspots of biodiversity, form the borders between countries, and at height their snow and glaciers melt to give fresh water — that is why they are called 'water towers'.

Europe and Africa

The European Plain — among the largest plains in the world; fertile soil — wheat, barley, rye, potatoes (a big share of the world's wheat comes from Europe); coal, oil, gas; Moscow, Berlin, Paris, Amsterdam, Warsaw; the site of many wars. The Alps — 1,200 km, crescent-shaped; Mont Blanc; the flower edelweiss (national flower of Austria, in the sunflower family); they feed the Rhine and Danube. The Sahara — the largest hot desert; 30–46°C (sometimes 50°C+), rainfall under 25 cm; oases, date palms, cactus, the big-eared fennec fox, underground homes; old gold-and-salt trade routes. The Savannah — a tropical grassland; elephants, lions, zebras, giraffes; wet and dry seasons. The Nile — the longest river in the world, ~6,650 km; it nurtured the Egyptian civilisation.

South America

The Andes — the continent's largest mountain chain (west coast); Aconcagua — the highest peak in the Western Hemisphere; Bolivia's Uyuni — the world's largest salt flat; copper, zinc, silver, lithium; only 1/4 of the original plants and animals remain — a conservation hotspot; the Andean condor (wingspan 3.2 m). The Amazon — the river with the largest volume of water; from the Andes of Peru to the Atlantic; the Amazon rainforest — the 'Lungs of the Earth', ~400 tribal communities; interesting — dust from the Sahara blows across to bring phosphorus to the Amazon! The Atacama (Chile) — one of the driest places in the world, ~1,000 km; 40°C by day, 5°C at night; a great place for stargazing; NASA tested Mars rovers there; but there is also a heap of discarded fast fashion clothes — the UN called it an 'environmental and social emergency'.

North America and Australia-Antarctica

The Colorado Plateau (USA) — 3,30,000 sq km; layers of sedimentary rock, and erosion over millions of years carved the Grand Canyon (~5 million tourists a year); coal, uranium. The Rockies — 4,800 km; home of Native Americans, now in 'reservations'. Yellowstone — the Old Faithful geyser; in 1995 wolves were released, which reduced the elk population and the vegetation came back — ecosystem restoration. The Appalachians — among the oldest mountains in the world; the Cherokee community. The Great Lakes — 5 huge freshwater lakes; a problem of pollution. Australia — mostly the Great Australian Desert (Great Victoria Desert); dingoes, wallabies, wild camels (brought from India and Afghanistan in the 19th century); the Spinifex people have lived there for 25,000 years. Antarctica — the largest and coldest desert in the world (14 million sq km, more than 4 times the size of India)!

Looking back — two revolts in Odisha (Paika and Sambalpur)

Paika Sangram (1817), Khorda (Odisha): even after helping the Company in 1803, the Raja's territory was taken away, along with control of the Jagannath temple. Rajguru Jayee Rajguru was hanged in 1806. The Paikas (traditional peasant-soldiers) were taxed on their rent-free land, salt became expensive, and the cowrie currency was stopped. Buxi Jagabandhu, with the Paikas and tribal people, captured Puri; Lord Jagannath became a symbol of Odia identity. It was suppressed by May 1817, but Buxi continued guerrilla warfare and surrendered in 1825. Sambalpur Uprising (1827–1862) — the British did not recognise Surendra Sai as king and annexed the kingdom. After escaping from jail in 1857, he launched guerrilla attacks from hill forts with his brothers Udanta Sai and Chhabila Sai; at Kudopali (Dec 1857), 57 warriors were martyred along with Chhabila Sai. Surendra Sai, captured by deceit, spent 37 years in prison and died there.

Theme B · Tapestry of the Past

India's Long Road to Independence

From 1858 to 1947 — the reformers, the Congress, Swadeshi, the revolutionaries, Gandhi's movements, Bose's INA, Quit India, and Independence along with Partition.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

So many languages, so many religions — yet one!

Imagine a country with hundreds of languages, different religions and customs — and yet all of them together challenge the most powerful empire in the world. This is the story of India's freedom. The ICHR's 'Dictionary of Martyrs' lists the names of about 14,000 martyrs — this chapter names very few of them, but those who are not named are no less great. Everyone had one goal, but the paths were different: the Moderates, the Nationalists, the revolutionaries, Gandhi's satyagraha.

The 'Royal Proclamation' of 1858 — promises and reality

In 1858 the government came under the name of Queen Victoria: a Secretary of State for India in London, and a Viceroy (Governor General) in India. It promised respect for about 560 princes (but the 'paramountcy' belonged to the British), equal care for all subjects, and no forced conversion to Christianity. The reality: a Resident interfered in the princes' courts; exploitation and famines continued; Indians were paid less and had separate club and train compartments; in 1878 came the Arms Act (Indians could not keep weapons, the British could) and the Vernacular Press Act (censorship of newspapers in Indian languages).

Social reformers — change from within

Brahmo Samaj (1828, Calcutta) — Raja Ram Mohan Roy: opposed child marriage and caste discrimination, and fought for widows' rights and girls' education. Arya Samaj (1875, Bombay) — Swami Dayananda Saraswati: 'Go back to the Vedas', opposed birth-based caste, supported widow remarriage and women's education. The Shuddhi movement. And Iswar Chandra Vidyasagar, Syed Ahmad Khan, Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule — schools for girls and the backward classes.

Two inspirations — 'Vande Mataram' and Vivekananda

Bankim Chandra wrote 'Bande Mataram' in 1875 (Bengali has no 'va' sound, hence 'Ba') and added it to Anandamath in 1882. Tagore sang it at the 1896 Congress session; in 1950 it became the national song. Swami Vivekananda (Narendranath Datta, born 1863; guru Sri Ramakrishna) gave a powerful speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893; founded the Ramakrishna Mission; said 'Arise, awake and stop not till the goal is reached'; advised Jamsetji Tata to set up a research institute — which later became the Indian Institute of Science. His birthday, 12 January, is National Youth Day.

The birth of the Congress and growing anger

The British officer A.O. Hume quit his job, and in 1885 the first session of the Indian National Congress was held in Bombay — with Dadabhai Naoroji beside him, W.C. Bonnerjee as the first president, and 72 delegates. In the beginning the Congress was 'loyal' to British rule and asked only for small reforms — the British mostly ignored it. Anger grew: the Kuka/Namdhari movement (Baba Ram Singh, Punjab) — boycott of foreign goods, its own post and courts (even before the later boycotts!); in 1872, 65 Kukas were blown from cannons without trial, and Baba Ram Singh was sent to Burma. In 1897, over the oppression during the plague in Pune, the Chapekar brothers killed the Plague Commissioner. Tilak awakened the people through the newspaper 'Kesari', the Ganesh Chaturthi and Shivaji festivals — he became 'Lokmanya'; author of 'Gita Rahasya'. Birsa Munda — Ulgulan (1899–1900), the 'Great Tumult'; he died in jail at the age of 24.

1905 — the Partition of Bengal and Swadeshi

Viceroy Curzon divided Bengal in 1905 — the excuse was 'better administration', the real aim was divide and rule (Muslim-majority east, Hindu-majority west). People tied rakhis on each other's wrists and sang Vande Mataram on the streets (the British banned it!). Two weapons: Swadeshi (goods made in one's own country) and Boycott (bonfires of foreign cloth and sugar; boycott of government schools and courts). Swadeshi enterprise had begun earlier: Jamsetji Tata — Swadeshi Mills 1886; Punjab National Bank 1895 (Lala Lajpat Rai); P.C. Ray — Bengal Chemicals 1901. 1906: Bipin Chandra Pal's newspaper 'Bande Mataram' (editor Aurobindo) — 'absolute national autonomy'; the Bengal National College (principal Aurobindo; now Jadavpur University); the Calcutta Congress (Naoroji) adopted Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott and National Education. 'Bharat Mata' — a painting by Abanindranath Tagore, and the poems of Subramania Bharati. The same year the All-India Muslim League was formed in Dhaka. In 1911 the partition was cancelled, and the capital moved from Calcutta to Delhi.

Surat 1907 — Moderates vs Nationalists

At the Surat session (1907) the Congress split into two. The Moderates (Naoroji, Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee, Rashbehari Ghosh) wanted small reforms and slow self-rule within the British Empire. The Nationalists — Lal-Bal-Pal (Lala Lajpat Rai 'Lion of Punjab', Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal) and Aurobindo — wanted complete freedom. The British called them 'Extremists', but they called themselves simply 'Nationalists'.

The revolutionaries — lives on the line

Anushilan Samiti (1902), Abhinav Bharat (1904, V.D. Savarkar), the newspaper 'Yugantar'. 1908: Khudiram Bose (18 years old) and Prafulla Chaki threw a bomb at Judge Kingsford (by mistake two British women were killed); Khudiram was hanged — smiling. Alipore Bomb Case — C.R. Das fought Aurobindo's case, and Aurobindo was acquitted; Barin was sent to the Cellular Jail. Tilak got 6 years in Mandalay (lawyer Jinnah). Tilak: 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.' In 1910 Aurobindo went to Pondicherry — 'Sri Aurobindo', integral yoga. 1909 Nashik (Anant Kanhere), 1911 Vanchinathan (for V.O. Chidambaram Pillai's Swadeshi Steam Navigation), 1912 a bomb thrown at Viceroy Hardinge in Delhi. Ghadar Party (America; Lala Hardayal, Sohan Singh Bhakna) — tried to start a revolt in the army in 1914–15; Kartar Singh Sarabha (19) was hanged. Rash Behari Bose went to Japan. Bagha Jatin — a 75-minute gun battle at Balasore on 9 Sept 1915. Cellular Jail ('Kala Pani') — solitary confinement in the Andamans, prisoners forced to extract oil by hand.

The North-East's contribution

Khasi revolt (1829–33) — Tirot Sing; Singpho revolt (Assam); in 1857 Maniram Dewan and Piyali Baruah were hanged; in 1942 Kanaklata Barua and Kushal Konwar became martyrs; Manipur's Rani Gaidinliu fought the British from the age of 13.

Home Rule and the arrival of Gandhi

1916: the Congress was united again at Lucknow; Lucknow Pact — the Congress accepted the Muslim League's demand for separate electorates. The Home Rule Movement of Tilak and Annie Besant — self-rule within the empire. M.K. Gandhi (born 1869, Porbandar) faced racial discrimination in South Africa and developed satyagraha ('insistence on truth' — non-violence + willingness to suffer); he returned to India in 1915. Local satyagrahas: Champaran (Bihar, indigo farmers), Ahmedabad mill workers, Kheda (land revenue). Bardoli 1928 — Vallabhbhai Patel → 'Sardar'.

1919 — the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh

After World War I, instead of freedom came the Rowlatt Act (1919) — the police could jail anyone without trial. 13 April 1919, Baisakhi, Amritsar: General Dyer blocked the main exit and, on a peaceful crowd, ordered 1,650 rounds fired in about 10 minutes; people jumped into the well. The British figure was about 400 dead and 1,200+ injured; Indian estimates say 1,000+. Dyer = the 'Butcher of Amritsar'. Tagore returned his knighthood. Britain has not apologised to this day. Twenty years later Udham Singh killed O'Dwyer in London.

Non-Cooperation and Khilafat (1920–22)

Gandhi first held fasts and prayers against the Rowlatt Act, then launched Non-Cooperation — boycott of foreign goods, schools and courts, and khadi (hand-spun, hand-woven cloth) — 'Swaraj in one year'. Tilak died in Aug 1920; the movement was formally launched at the Nagpur session (Dec 1920). Khilafat (1919) — the Ali brothers and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad; in support of the Caliph of Turkey; Gandhi joined it for the sake of Hindu-Muslim unity. Jinnah left the Congress in 1920 (do not mix religion with freedom). Feb 1922, Chauri Chaura (UP) — a mob burned a police station and killed policemen → Gandhi called off the movement (Motilal, Jawaharlal, C.R. Das and Subhas were upset). Motilal and Das formed the Swaraj Party. In 1924 Turkey itself abolished the Caliphate.

Simon Go Back, Bhagat Singh and Purna Swaraj

The 1927 Simon Commission — not a single Indian member → 'Simon Go Back!'. At Lahore in 1928 Lala Lajpat Rai was hit with lathis — 'Every blow struck on me will be the last nail in the coffin of British rule'; he died two weeks later. Kakori (1925) — Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan and Chandrashekhar Azad of the HRA looted a government treasury; Bismil and Ashfaq were hanged. HSRA — Bhagat Singh threw harmless bombs in the Assembly 'to make the deaf hear'; in 1931 Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged (aged 22–23). Women students joined too — Suniti Chowdhury, Shanti Ghosh, Bina Das. 1928 Nehru Report — Dominion status. Dec 1929 Lahore session — the resolution for Purna Swaraj (complete independence) (Jawaharlal Nehru).

Salt that shook the government — the Dandi March

The government held a monopoly on salt and charged a heavy tax. March–April 1930 — Gandhi's 380 km Dandi March (south Gujarat) and a fistful of salt — Civil Disobedience began. Thousands were arrested; women came out from behind the veil. Sarojini Naidu — the first Indian woman president of the Congress (1925). Peshawar, 23 April 1930, Qissa Khwani Bazaar — firing on a peaceful crowd after Abdul Ghaffar Khan was arrested; the Garhwal Rifles under Chandra Singh Garhwali refused to fire. 1931 Gandhi-Irwin Pact → Round Table Conference (London). 1934 the movement was withdrawn. 1935 Government of India Act — provincial autonomy. Poona Pact — Ambedkar wanted a separate electorate, Gandhi went on a fast; the compromise: reserved seats were increased.

The two-nation idea, World War II and Netaji

In 1930 Muhammad Iqbal spoke of a separate region for Muslims in the north-west; the 1940 Lahore Resolution (Jinnah) demanded Muslim-majority states. The Congress's Maulana Azad and Abdul Ghaffar Khan opposed the two-nation theory. World War II (1939) — India was dragged in without being asked; 2.5 million+ Indian soldiers (the largest volunteer army in history), 87,000 died; on Churchill's orders grain was taken out → the 1943 Bengal famine, in which millions died. Subhas Chandra Bose — in 1941 he escaped house arrest and went through Afghanistan and Russia to Germany, then to Singapore in 1943: the Indian National Army (INA) and the Azad Hind government; they fought in Burma, Manipur and Nagaland, and lost (1944).

Quit India — 'Do or Die' (1942)

The Cripps Mission (1942) — dominion status after the war, but provinces could separate → rejected. August 1942 — Gandhi: 'Do or Die', the British must leave at once. All the top leaders were arrested, yet the people kept the movement going; Ram Manohar Lohia and Jayaprakash Narayan led it underground. Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the flag at Bombay's Gowalia Tank Maidan. Assam's Kanaklata Barua (17) was martyred while raising the flag at Gohpur police station — 'Birangana'. Viceroy Linlithgow: 'the most serious revolt since 1857'. Over 1 lakh jailed, about 10,000 killed.

Independence and Partition (1947)

After 1945 Britain was exhausted; in Jan 1946 the Air Force and in Feb 1946 the Royal Indian Navy (Bombay) mutinied — trust in the army was gone. INA trials. Cabinet Mission 1946 — deadlock. 16 Aug 1946 — Jinnah's 'Direct Action Day': 'either a divided India or a destroyed India'; terrible riots in Calcutta. Sept 1946 — Nehru's Interim Government. Mountbatten gave Cyril Radcliffe only 5 weeks to draw the border — the Radcliffe Line tore Punjab and Bengal apart; Pakistan = West Pakistan + East Pakistan (Bangladesh since 1971). Millions were displaced, with terrible violence. 15 August 1947 — freedom at midnight; Nehru: 'Tryst with destiny'. Sardar Patel brought 560+ princely states into India by persuasion (and, where needed, the army) — 'Iron Man of India'. Freedom did not come from one person or one method — Gandhi, the revolutionaries, revolts, army mutinies, Britain's weakness after the World War — all together.

Theme C · Our Cultural Heritage and Knowledge Traditions

A Journey Through Indian Architecture

From the cities of Harappa to colonial buildings — stupas, caves, stepwells, temples, mosques, forts, tombs, havelis and churches.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is architecture?

Architecture = the design and construction of buildings, which should be both functional and aesthetic (beautiful). It is an art as well as a highly technical science — and India has been skilled at it for thousands of years. Let's go on a journey — at every stop a special kind of building. Remember one fun fact: no old building in this chapter used cement!

The beginning — the smart cities of Harappa

The Harappan civilisation is the earliest example of town planning in the Indian subcontinent — streets laid out by direction, a bathing area in almost every house, and a network of drains for dirty water. Most cities were built of brick (the river plains had plenty of clay). In a large house at Mohenjo-daro (Sindh), servants probably lived below and the owners above — away from dust and noise! Dholavira (Rann of Kachchh) used stone (found right there); the highest part, the 'Castle', had a gateway, a guard room, a polished stone pillar and large reservoirs.

Stupa — a dome in memory of the Buddha

From the 4th/3rd century BCE, stupas were built along with Buddhism — dome-like structures that held the relics of the Buddha or of monks. In the beginning the Buddha was not shown in human form but through symbols — footprints, the dharma chakra, or the stupa. The main stupa at Sanchi (MP) was started by Ashoka; it has four large toranas (decorated gateways showing scenes of towns and villages) and a pradakshina path inside the vedika (railing). At Bharhut (MP) there are scenes of stupa worship and the names of donors. Jainism has stupas too.

Caves cut out of hills

Rock-cut architecture = cutting straight into rock to make a cave — durable and useful. Udayagiri–Khandagiri (near Bhubaneswar) — from the 2nd century BCE, mostly for Jain monks to live in; one cave has Kharavela's inscription. Maharashtra's Ajanta and Ellora — the Kailasha temple was carved by chipping away thousands of tonnes of stone from a single rock! Ellora's Cave 10 — a large Buddha statue beside a stupa, the roof carved to look like wooden beams; in the side passage monks did pradakshina (walking clockwise around a sacred object) — this tradition later entered temples too. Imagine all this done with only hammers and chisels!

Some special temples

Sharada Peetha (J&K) — goddess Sharada (Saraswati), a centre of learning from the 6th to 12th century, the Sharada script; that is why Kashmir is called 'Sharada Desh'. Hidimba Devi Temple (Manali, 16th century) — Bhima's wife Hidimba; nearby is the temple of their son Ghatotkacha. Jageshwar (Almora, Uttarakhand) — about 124 stone temples, 8th–18th century. Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) — Guru Ramdas laid the foundation, and Guru Arjan Dev carried the construction forward in 1588; the holiest place of the Sikhs.

Water structures — wisdom of a monsoon land

Because of the monsoon, India developed unique ways of storing water — from village ponds to the Grand Anicut. The Harappans built wells with trapezoid-shaped bricks that could not cave inward — the bricks locked into one another! Shringaverpur (UP; the Ramayana's Shringaverapura, where Ram, Sita and Lakshman crossed the Ganga) — linked reservoirs filled by Ganga floods; in the first section the silt settled. Stepwell (vav/baori) — Gujarat and Rajasthan; Rani ki Vav — many storeys, hundreds of sculptures; people got water and also sat in the cool space to chat! Temples had a pushkarini (tank) (Vijayanagara). Today many are victims of pollution and neglect.

The temple's structure — like a human body

Classical temples began in the Gupta period; the rules are in the Shilpashastra. Parts: the entrance (gopuram — south; torana — north), the mandapa (hall with pillars), and the garbhagriha (the innermost room, where the murti is). Above the garbhagriha rises the spire: shikhara in the north, vimana in the south. The orientation is usually east–west, with the garbhagriha facing east. The temple takes the form of the Purusha (the cosmic being) — the spire is the head, the platform the feet. Entering a temple = a journey from the outer world to the inner world. A large boundary wall = prakara. Besides worship, temples were also centres of education, art, dance and music, society and trade.

Four famous temples

Brihadishvara (Thanjavur) — Rajaraja Chola I, about 1010; a pyramid-like vimana over 60 m high; a giant Nandi carved from a single stone; carvings on granite. Lingaraja (Bhubaneswar) — early 11th century; 3 large mandapas; sandstone and laterite. Both are dedicated to Shiva. Dilwara (Mount Abu) — 5 Jain temples, 11th–13th century, Solanki/Chaulukya; white marble from the Aravallis, carving on every surface. Bishnupur (West Bengal) — 17th–18th century terracotta (baked clay) temples; scenes of Radha-Krishna and Ram; shaped by hand and baked in kilns. These needed mathematics and advanced geometry — like the pyramids of Egypt or the Gothic cathedrals of Europe.

The structure of a mosque

In a mosque the prayer hall faces Mecca; there is a dome and a minaret (from where the muezzin gives the call to prayer). In the wall is the mihrab — a niche showing the direction of Mecca. A large mosque for Friday prayers = a Jama Masjid (jama = gathering, masjid = mosque). Ahmedabad Jama Masjid — Sultan Ahmed Shah I, 15th century; yellow sandstone; temple-like pillars and jaali work — with the help of Hindu and Jain craftsmen; in an earthquake the upper parts of the minarets fell. The Sidi Saiyyed jaali is famous. This is called the Indo-Islamic style — domes, arches (pointed and corbelled), jaali, flower-and-leaf designs; in Rajasthan there are also jharokhas and chhatris. Delhi Jama Masjid — the largest in India; Shah Jahan (mid-17th century, Shahjahanabad); about 5,000 workers; red sandstone + white marble; 3 gateways, 2 minarets of 40 m, 3 domes; the courtyard holds 25,000 people.

Forts and palaces

Forts stood near important routes, often on hills. Mehrangarh (Jodhpur) — from the mid-15th century; red sandstone; palaces inside, women's and men's quarters, stables; now a museum. In front is the Jaswant Thada — a cenotaph (a memorial where the person is not buried). Jodhpur's blue houses — copper sulphate in the lime wash, to keep termites away! Agra Red Fort — rebuilt by Akbar; red sandstone from Dholpur; Shah Jahan added marble palaces (Musamman Burj — where Aurangzeb kept him imprisoned, Khas Mahal); river water was lifted by a Persian wheel into a tank, then sent through clay pipes to fountains — an 'eco-friendly AC'! Orchha (MP, on the Betwa) — Bundela Rajputs, 16th century; Raja Mahal, Jahangir Mahal, Rai Praveen Mahal; the Ram Raja temple. Mysuru Palace (Amba Vilas) — the Wadiyars; early 20th century (the old wooden palace burned down); a golden throne at Dasara; about 1 lakh bulbs.

Tombs — Humayun and the Taj

In Islam and Christianity the dead are buried, so mausoleums (large tombs) were built; in Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism the dead are cremated — which is why there are no tombs of ancient kings. Tombs have symmetry and gardens too. Humayun's Tomb (Delhi) — built by his wife Bega Begum in 1558; a Persian architect; Dara Shikoh's grave is here too; a six-pointed star — the Jewish 'Star of David', in Islam the 'Seal of Solomon', in Hindu tantra the union of Purusha and Prakriti. Taj Mahal — Shah Jahan, for Mumtaz Mahal, mid-17th century; a meeting of three cultures: the charbagh and large arches (Persian), Quranic calligraphy (Arabic), chhatris, lotus and jaali (Indian); coloured stones set in marble (inlay). In the 1970s it turned yellow from pollution → a PIL in the Supreme Court, and in the mid-1990s industries nearby were restricted.

Houses, churches and the British period

Old wealthy houses had a courtyard in the middle with rooms all around — this design was there in the large Harappan houses too (4,500 years ago)! Shekhawati havelis (Rajasthan) — colourful frescoes; many are now hotels or museums. Chettinad houses (Tamil Nadu) — built by merchants who traded with Southeast Asia; teak from Burma, marble from Italy; papads and chillies dried in the courtyard! Churches: some in European style — St. Philomena's (Mysuru), Gothic; in Kerala, local forms — St. Thomas Orthodox Church, Mulanthuruthy (a temple-like pillar in front), Kalloopara (sloping tiled roof). Indo-Saracenic (British Raj, mid-19th–20th century) — Islamic + Hindu + Gothic: Madras High Court (1892), Victoria Memorial (Kolkata), Mysuru Palace. Our Fundamental Duty: 'to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture'.

The Role of the Judiciary in Our Society

What justice is, the link between law and justice, the structure of the Supreme Court–High Court–District Court system, PIL, tribunals and Lok Adalat.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Someone wronged you — now what?

Imagine someone stole your tiffin. You would want them to be punished, to apologise, or at least to give your tiffin back. But the person accused would say — 'first listen to my side!' This is the heart of justice. Our Preamble promises every citizen social, economic and political justice. The judiciary is one of the three pillars of government — independent of the legislature and the executive. It settles disputes according to law, checks that the laws made by Parliament stay within the Constitution, and sees that the executive does not cross its limits.

The link between law and justice

As societies grew, the job of delivering justice passed to the government. A law = general rules made by the government; breaking them leads to a fine, compensation or jail. The system for making complaints and presenting one's case = the judicial system. After Independence, more than 1,500 discriminatory British laws were repealed — such as the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 (which treated some communities as criminals by birth) and the Vernacular Press Act, 1878. Other countries had such laws too: the USA's 'Jim Crow laws' (separate water taps and swimming pools for Black people — ended in the 1960s) and South Africa's apartheid (ended in 1991).

Two sides of justice

Substantive justice — the outcome should be right: is the decision truly just and fair? Procedural justice — the process should be right: before a decision, every side must get a full chance to be heard. And one big rule: every person is presumed innocent until proved guilty.

The ladder of courts

India has a unified judicial system — the Supreme Court at the top, High Courts at the state level, and Subordinate/District Courts at the district level. Just as in school you go from class teacher → principal → board, if you are not satisfied with a lower court's decision you can make an appeal to a higher one.

The Supreme Court — the country's highest court

Established on 26 January 1950 (the day the Constitution came into force); Article 124; in New Delhi. The final interpreter of the Constitution. Motto: 'Yato dharmastato jayah' — where there is dharma, there is victory. Head = the Chief Justice of India; total strength 33 + 1 (CJI + 33 judges). Appointed by the President. Qualifications: an Indian citizen + (a High Court judge for 5 years, or a High Court advocate for 10 years, or in the President's view a distinguished jurist). Retirement at 65 years.

Writ and PIL — the power of the common person

If Fundamental Rights (equality, freedom, life, freedom of speech, freedom of religion) are violated, a citizen can file a writ petition — in the Supreme Court (Article 32) or a High Court (Article 226). PIL (Public Interest Litigation) — a new tool of the Supreme Court: anyone can approach the court on a matter of public interest, to give meaning to Article 21 (right to life). The first PIL: the lawyer Kapila Hingorani read in a newspaper that undertrial prisoners in Bihar had been in jail longer than their possible sentence → the Supreme Court got them released and issued guidelines for about 40,000 undertrials across the country. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India — cleaning the Ganga, protecting the Taj Mahal. Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) — guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace → the 2013 law.

The Supreme Court's 'doors' (jurisdiction)

Original (Art. 131) — only in the Supreme Court: Centre vs State, or disputes between states (such as river water). Appellate — appeals against High Court decisions (interpretation of the Constitution or a major question of law; cases involving the death penalty — Art. 132–134A); also from tribunals like the NGT and NCLAT. Advisory (Art. 143) — if the President seeks advice, a Constitution Bench of at least 5 judges gives its opinion. Special Leave (Art. 136) — an appeal, by special permission, against the decision of any court or tribunal. The Supreme Court's decision is binding on all courts (Art. 141); it can pass any order for 'complete justice' (Art. 142); and all authorities must assist it (Art. 144).

The High Court and the lower courts

Article 214 — a High Court in every state; but there are 25 High Courts for 28 states, because some are shared — like Gauhati (Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal), Bombay (Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra-Nagar Haveli & Daman-Diu), Punjab & Haryana (+ Chandigarh), Madras (+ Puducherry), Calcutta (+ Andaman-Nicobar), Kerala (+ Lakshadweep). Judges: appointed by the President after consulting the CJI, the Governor and the Chief Justice of that HC; qualification — 10 years in a judicial post or as an HC advocate (Art. 217); retirement at 62. Functions: writs (226), appeals from lower courts, and supervision over them (Art. 227). Subordinate courts (Art. 233–237) — District, Sessions, Revenue and Small Causes courts; this is where the judiciary is closest to ordinary people. The District Judge is appointed by the Governor in consultation with the HC.

Tribunals — special courts for special disputes

A tribunal = a quasi-judicial body that settles a particular kind of dispute: NCLT (companies and insolvency), Consumer Fora (consumers), the National Green Tribunal (environment), APTEL (electricity), the Armed Forces Tribunal, the Central Administrative Tribunal (service matters of government employees). Example: a family paid a builder about ₹65 lakh for a flat; for 4 years there was no sign of the building → the National Consumer Commission ordered the builder to return the money with interest and the cost of the case.

New laws and the digital court

The old British laws were replaced: Indian Penal Code 1860 → Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023; CrPC 1973 → Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023; Indian Evidence Act 1872 → Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 — for faster justice, digital evidence and better protection of victims. Digital steps: e-Filing, hybrid hearings, live streaming, translation of judgments into many languages, and the National Judicial Data Grid (anyone can check the details of any case). From 2024–25 there is a 'Virtual Justice Clock' at the Supreme Court gate. A litigant = a party in a case (both the one who files the case and the one it is filed against).

Settling disputes without going to court — ADR

To reduce the load on courts there is Alternate Dispute Resolution: Arbitration — both sides accept an independent arbitrator (a retired judge, lawyer or expert); the decision is final and binding (Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996). Mediation — a mediator only helps the parties reach a settlement and cannot impose a decision (Mediation Act 2023). Lok Adalat — the 'people's court', by mutual consent; for cases already in court and also before going to court (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987). Gram Nyayalaya — a village-level mobile court with simple procedure (Gram Nyayalayas Act 2008). And yes — citizens can also raise their voice for the rights of people, animals and nature through a PIL.

Citizenship: Rights and Duties

Who is a citizen, the 6 Fundamental Rights, the 11 Fundamental Duties, recognising discrimination, and taking everyone along (inclusion).

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Kruti's story — who is a good citizen?

A new student, Kruti, was being teased about her accent. Sonali and Harshada stopped the bullies and invited Kruti to join their badminton team. This is what a good citizen does — stands up against wrong and includes everyone. A citizen = a person who is legally a member of the country and can take part in its political system (decision-making). Citizenship = legal identity + social responsibility. Note: being a resident somewhere is different from being a citizen — not every resident is a citizen, and a citizen can live abroad. The rules: the Citizenship Act 1955 and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019.

The fair's story — what are rights?

At a city fair: Meena was stopped from joining a debate ('girls will not speak on stage'); Ravi, the son of a cart vendor, was not allowed into the library stall while a rich boy was welcomed; some children were cleaning instead of being in school; and for Gaganjit, who uses a wheelchair, there was no ramp. The teacher explained that the Constitution gives everyone Fundamental Rights. The children spoke to the organisers — Meena got into the debate, Ravi into the stall, a ramp was built, and the children went back to school! Rights = things necessary to live with dignity, safety and respect — 'justified claims'. Something that harms others or oneself is not a right.

The 6 Fundamental Rights

The Constitution has six fundamental rights — they can be enforced in court, and they protect you even against wrong actions by the government: (1) Equality (Art. 14–18) — everyone is equal before the law; no discrimination on grounds of religion, caste, sex or place of birth; untouchability abolished (17); no titles except military or academic (18). (2) Freedom (19–22) — speech, movement, peaceful assembly; but with reasonable restrictions. (3) Against exploitation (23–24). (4) Freedom of religion (25–28). (5) Cultural & educational (29–30). (6) Constitutional remedies (32).

Equality vs Equity — three children and a wall

Imagine three children of different heights watching a match over a wall. Equality — give everyone the same stool; the tall one can see, the short one still cannot. Equity — give each one help according to need; the shortest gets two stools. Because some people are behind due to historical discrimination, equity is needed to bring them to equality — like Beti Bachao Beti Padhao.

The right to life — more than just breathing

Article 21 — life and liberty; this means living with dignity: livelihood, education, health, a clean environment, fair treatment. Children with disabilities should get equal chances to learn and play (for example a tactile map for visually impaired children). Article 21A — free and compulsory education for ages 6–14. Interesting fact: New Zealand's Whanganui river has been legally recognised as 'living'; Costa Rica's constitution has rights of nature. In 2017 the Uttarakhand High Court called the Ganga and Yamuna 'legal entities', but the Supreme Court overturned this, calling it 'legally unsustainable'.

Against exploitation — Childline 1098

Exploitation = taking unfair advantage of someone — bonded labour (forced work without fair wages), human trafficking (buying and selling people), exploitation of women and children, child labour. The rule: children below 14 cannot be employed; they may help only in non-hazardous family work, during holidays or after school; never in factories, mines or restaurants. Childline 1098 — a countdown from 10! It began in the late 1990s from the project of an educational institute. In 2015, on one phone call, 20 children were rescued from a factory doing embroidery on clothes; the guilty were arrested.

Religion, culture and the right that is the 'soul'

Freedom of religion — everyone may choose, practise and propagate their religion; no one can be forced to follow a religion or to pay for one; government or government-aided schools cannot promote any one religion. Cultural and educational rights — communities may protect their language, script and traditions; admission to government institutions cannot be denied on grounds of religion, caste or language; religious and linguistic minorities may run their own schools (some people see this as in conflict with secularism — the debate continues). Constitutional remedies (Art. 32) — if a right is violated, go straight to the Supreme Court. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar: this is the 'soul and heart' of the Constitution.

Cleaning Green Park — what are duties?

One Sunday the people of Green Park Colony began cleaning up together — children picked up wrappers, drains were cleared, plants were planted, good messages appeared on walls, and water and food were kept out for dogs. A reporter asked a little girl why. Her answer: 'Taking care of our neighbourhood is our duty.' Fundamental Duties were not in the Constitution at first; in 1976, 10 were added in Article 51A (Part IV-A); in 2002 came the 11th — parents must ensure education for children aged 6–14.

The 11 Fundamental Duties — each with an example

(1) Respect the Constitution, the national flag and national anthem — stand straight for the anthem at assembly. (2) Follow the ideals of the freedom struggle — speaking out against bullying is also fighting injustice. (3) Uphold the unity, integrity and sovereignty of the country — learn words from each other's languages. (4) Defend the country and help in disasters — NCC cadets in a flood. (5) Brotherhood and respect for women. (6) Preserve culture and heritage — don't write your name on a fort wall! (7) Protect the environment and show compassion to animals. (8) Develop scientific temper and curiosity — teach how to purify water. (9) Protect public property and give up violence — don't break benches when you lose a match. (10) Strive for excellence in every task. (11) Provide education for children aged 6–14.

Ancient India — dharma, rina, adhikara

The word dharma is often translated as 'duty', but its meaning is much wider — whatever is good for the individual and for society. Rina (debt) — from birth we owe a 'debt' to the gods, to ancestors and parents, to teachers, to fellow human beings and to nature; repaying it is a duty. Adhikara in ancient texts meant capability or eligibility — the right to do the work one is fit for. Today the Constitution gives rights equally to all.

Rights and duties — two sides of a coin

Rights are protected by law (you can go to court); duties are moral and social responsibilities (failing to do them does not always lead to legal action). But with every right comes a duty: I have a right to respect → it is my duty to respect others; I have freedom of speech → I must listen to others' opinions and speak without harming anyone. The right to education → to study and to let others study!

Discrimination — visible and invisible

Sanya was told she did not 'look right' for the lead role — but she auditioned and was chosen; judging by looks is discrimination. Discrimination = unfair treatment because of caste, religion, race, disability, appearance, sex or economic status — unethical and illegal. It can also be hidden: not taking someone in a group project, not letting someone sit next to you on the bus, taunting. Calling Rohan 'Drummy' because he is overweight; leaving Mina out because she couldn't pay for the trip, saying she is 'not of our status'. Hidden discrimination is hard to prove. Inspiration: Savitribai Phule — people threw mud at her when she opened a school for girls, yet she did not stop; Anandibai Joshi — married at 9, lost her baby, then went to America (Pennsylvania) and became a doctor.

Inclusion — leave no one behind

Mrs. Amita noticed that the library books were hardly being borrowed; when she started a reading club, only children who were good at English came. She added books, stories and puppets in the local language — now all the children take part! Inclusion = an environment where everyone feels welcome, respected and valued and has equal opportunity — going beyond just reservation: braille books, ramps, help in the classroom, ways of talking to people who speak other languages. Wait for the classmate who learns slowly, give them a chance to speak — that is inclusion.

Dynamics of Population

What demography is — birth rate, fertility, death rate, migration, the 3 stages of demographic transition, the population pyramid and India's demographic dividend.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

Demography — more than just 'counting' people

How many people are there in the world? How many are children, how many are elderly, how many boys and girls, how many are educated, who does what work? And how does all this change over time — through births, deaths and people moving from one place to another? This study is called demography. Its parts: size (how many), structure and composition (age, sex, education…), pattern and trend (change). Why it matters: more children → more schools; more elderly people → more hospitals; more young people → more jobs needed. Companies also design products by looking at age, sex and income. Working-age population = 15–64 years. In India the Census is generally held every 10 years — it records age, sex, marriage, education, language, religion, family, work and income.

The world and India — at a glance

In 2023 India overtook China — it is now the most populous country in the world; about 17% of the world's people are Indian (2024: India ~144 crore, China ~140 crore, USA ~33 crore). The world: 2.5 billion in 1950 → 8.2 billion in 2025 → 9.7 billion by 2050. According to the UN it will peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, and then fall. Why did growth speed up after the 1950s? Antibiotics, vaccines, ORS, clean water, and fewer child deaths from malaria and diarrhoea. The populations of China, Japan and Italy are shrinking; Nigeria and Ethiopia are growing fast. In 2025 Asia has about 4.8 billion people and Africa about 1.5 billion.

What decides population? — 4 'measurements'

Birth rate — live births per 1,000 people in a year. Fertility rate — the average number of children a woman will have in her lifetime. Death/mortality rate — deaths per 1,000 people in a year. Migration — people moving within or out of a country. Think of water in a bucket: births = water coming in from the tap, deaths = water leaking out through a hole, migration = someone pouring water in or taking it out!

The magic of 2.1 — replacement level

A fertility rate of 2.1 = the 'replacement level' — just enough children to take the place of their parents so that the population stays steady. Why 2.1 and not 2? Because some children die before growing up, and the ratio of boys to girls is not perfectly equal — so a little extra is needed. Above 2.1 = the population will grow; below = it will slowly shrink. When fertility falls, the birth rate falls too.

China's One-Child Policy — a lesson

In the 1970s China feared shortages of food, jobs and housing, so in 1979 it began the One-Child Policy — most families could have only one child; breaking the rule meant fines or losing a job. Fertility went from 7.5 (1963) → 1 (2025). The population peaked at 1.43 billion in 2022 and is now falling; by 2050 it will be 1.31 billion. The share of elderly people (65+) goes from 14% (2023) → 26% (2050) — fewer workers, a slower economy. A whole generation has no brothers or sisters, and the web of relatives (kinship network) has become smaller. The policy ended in 2015 and families can now have up to 3 children — yet fertility is still below 2.1! In India the slogan was 'Hum Do, Hamare Do' ('We two, our two' — since 1952); fertility went from 5.7 (1950) → 1.9 (2025).

Why is fertility falling?

The biggest reason — better education for girls. Educated women take up jobs, marry later and want fewer children. Living is expensive in cities, so families are smaller. Compare the states: Bihar — fertility 3.0, female literacy 55%; UP 2.4 / 66.1%; Jharkhand 2.3 / 61.7%; Karnataka 1.7 / 73.4%; Punjab 1.6 / 79.4%; Delhi 1.6 / 83.7% — where female literacy is higher, fertility is lower!

Death rate, lifespan and migration

A low death rate means good health and nutrition. In India child mortality (deaths of children under 5 per 1,000 births) went from 271 (1950) → 28 (2023)! Life expectancy (the average lifespan of a newborn) was only ~30 years at independence and ~70 years in 2024. This does not mean everyone will live exactly 70 years — it is an average. Migration — for jobs and studies; also because of disaster or unrest. Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru have grown hugely through migration — but this puts pressure on water, air, land, housing and transport. Think: when young people leave villages for cities, what will change in the villages?

Demographic transition — the 3 'ages' of a country

Just as a person goes through childhood, youth and old age, so does a country: Stage 1 — high birth rate and high death rate → population almost steady (India 1901–1921, poor health, low literacy). Stage 2 — death rate falls, birth rate is still high → rapid growth (India after 1951). Stage 3 — death rate is low, birth rate also starts falling (urbanisation, income, education, women working) → growth slows; if it falls too far, the population shrinks (Italy, Japan, South Korea). In India fertility has been falling since the mid-1980s and is now below replacement level.

How India is spread out, and the sex ratio

Share by state: UP ~16%, Maharashtra ~9%, Bihar ~9%, West Bengal ~8%, MP, TN and Rajasthan ~6% each… Sex ratio = number of women per 1,000 men. Census 2001: 933 → 2011: 943 (women are 48.5%). Kerala, Puducherry and Tamil Nadu are around or above 1,000. A balanced sex ratio is important for equal development and social stability.

Population pyramid — a picture of the population

A graph with men on the left, women on the right, children at the bottom, adults in the middle and the elderly at the top. A wide base = many children, a young and growing population (India earlier, Nigeria). A wide middle = mostly working people, a stable population. A wide top = an ageing population (Japan). In 1950 India had a narrow triangle (many children, median age 30–35). Now it is bell-shaped — narrow at the bottom (fewer children), thick in the middle (more people aged 15–60), and slowly thickening at the top. In the future India too will age, like Japan and China.

Demographic dividend — a limited 'window'

Youth bulge = a very large number of young people aged 16–30. In India about 27% are under 15 and about 65% are of working age (15–59). Median age (the middle age): India 28, Japan 50, Italy 48, Germany 47, China 40, USA 39. When more people work → more production and tax → schools, hospitals, roads. This is called the demographic dividend. But this window is limited: children under 5 peaked in 2007, and those under 15 in 2011; after 2050 India will begin to age. Kerala and Tamil Nadu already have more elderly people (than Bihar and UP). An ageing population means higher health spending, the burden of pensions and fewer taxpayers.

The importance of elders and preparing for the future

The Indian ashrama dharma — 4 stages of life: brahmacharya, grihastha, vanaprastha, sanyasa. Elders step back from work but guide family and society with wisdom, stories, customs and experience. Developed countries: Japan raised the retirement age and uses robots and AI to care for the elderly. Developing countries (India): invest in education, skills and health. Working women face a 'double burden' (job + home) — they need daycare, flexible hours and family support. Many countries have tried to raise fertility, but without much success.

Theme E · Economic Life Around Us

India's Urban Landscape

How cities form, why they are economic and cultural centres, their challenges, and good planning as in Jaipur and Singapore.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

City vs village — what is the difference?

India's urban tradition is very old — from the Harappan sites of Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi to Vijayanagara, Varanasi, Ujjain. Urban areas = larger populations, varied occupations and jobs, government institutions, and large transport and communication networks; they are run by elected local bodies (municipal councils/corporations). Rural areas = villages, smaller populations, close-knit communities, mostly farming; they are run by the gram panchayat. The definition of a city differs from country to country: Denmark — more than 220 people, Japan — 50,000, India — 5,000!

Population density — how many people in the room?

If a few friends come to your house, you can move around comfortably; but pack 30 people into the same room and it is crowded! How 'crowded' a place is gets measured by population density = total population ÷ area (sq km) — that is, the number of people per sq km. In the satellite photo of the Haryana village Asadpur Khera you see green-brown open fields; Gurugram shows a web of brown-grey buildings. Among the largest cities of 2025 are Delhi (~4.19 crore — at the top!) and Kolkata.

Why do cities grow?

In history, cities came up where there was a river (easy transport), a crossroads of trade routes (markets), and farm produce and raw materials nearby (craftspeople and industries). According to the UN, by 2050 68% of the world's people will live in cities (now 55%). In India the urban population went from 17% (1951) → ~40% (2025). The journey of urbanisation: a farming village → new factory, hotel, bank, government office → second and third sectors (industry, transport, services) → people arrive, more variety of work → small town/trading centre → metropolitan city (tall buildings, malls, metro).

How did Sakchi become Jamshedpur?

Sakchi, a small village on the Chota Nagpur Plateau, had iron ore nearby — so Asia's first steel plant was set up here. The Tata family dreamed of a city with modern facilities for its workers; Sir Dorabji Tata built a hospital even before steel was being made! In 1919 it was named Jamshedpur — today Jharkhand's largest industrial city, with wide tree-lined roads, gardens and open spaces.

Every city has its own identity

Mumbai — a port on the Arabian Sea; the Bombay Stock Exchange in 1875 → the country's economic capital; the film industry began in the 1910s and shone by the 1930s; Bandra-Worli Sea Link, Coastal Road, BKC. Kolkata — a port on the eastern coast. Bengaluru — the 'Silicon Valley of India'; software, startups, universities; even AI. Hyderabad — technology. Smaller specialised cities: Moradabad (brass), Ludhiana, Tirupur (textiles), Jaipur (handicrafts, gems and jewellery), Channapatna (wooden toys), Kanchipuram (silk sarees). Kochi, Indore, Lucknow, Nagpur — costs are lower, so companies open warehouses and offices there. Tourism/pilgrimage: Agra (the Taj — 70–80 lakh people a year), Amritsar, Varanasi.

Delhi and the NCR

Since the Iron Age Delhi has been a centre of culture, trade and administration — Rajput, Turkish and Mughal rulers built forts, roads and markets; the British built a new capital, railways and electricity. After independence ministries, industries and universities drew people in; pressure grew on housing, water and sewers. Hence the National Capital Region (NCR) — NOIDA (New Okhla Industrial Development Authority), Ghaziabad; highways and the metro. Since the 1990s Gurugram has been a hub of IT and BPO (Business Process Outsourcing — getting one company's work done by another company).

A city is a mix of cultures

When people from different regions arrive, their languages, foods and festivals mix. Vada pav — the Maharashtrian potato vada + pav (the Portuguese brought pav and potatoes!); a favourite of Mumbai's textile mill workers — easy to make, carry and eat. Kolkata — the Indian Museum, Victoria Memorial; Durga Puja — in 2021 it was added to UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list. Cities create libraries, museums, stadiums, music festivals; Delhi's Pradhanmantri Sangrahalaya (Prime Ministers' Museum) tells the story of the prime ministers.

The challenges of cities

More people → more demand for food, water, electricity and housing. Broken, narrow roads, unplanned settlements, traffic jams, 'heat islands' (cities hotter than villages), garbage. In the NCR the AQI is often above 400, while the safe limit is 50! (AQI tells you about pollutants like PM2.5, PM10, CO and SO₂ as one simple number — the higher it is, the more dangerous.) High density and economic inequality also lead to more crime. Slums — crowded, with makeshift houses and poor water and sanitation; they come up on empty land near railway lines and factories, because cheap housing is not available. Yet slum dwellers are the ones who sell vegetables, do housework, wash clothes, and work in construction and plumbing — they keep the city running! The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana — affordable housing for poor families.

Bengaluru's lakes — saving them together

Bengaluru faces water shortages in summer and floods in the rains — because of poor planning. Too much groundwater is pumped out, rainwater harvesting is not done, and lakes are encroached upon. There were once ~1,000 lakes, now there are ~200; many have sewage and chemicals; Bellandur and Varthur lakes became covered in foam, gave off toxic gas, and even caught fire! Now NGOs, RWAs (Resident Welfare Associations) and citizens together are removing silt, repairing bunds with natural materials, cleaning drains, planting native plants and bringing the fish back — as at Yelahanka Lake.

Jaipur — the planning of the 'Pink City' (1727)

Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II made Vidyadhar Bhattacharya the chief architect in 1727 — an expert in Vastu Shastra and Shilpa Shastra. The city is laid out in a grid pattern — rectangular blocks (chowkri), aligned to the directions. Wide roads, open spaces, gardens, 9 public squares (chowks) for trade, meeting and temples. For protection, a 20-foot high wall with 7 gates. The buildings on the main roads were made of pink sandstone — a uniform, beautiful look → the 'Pink City'. Sunlight, fresh air, water from lakes and canals (for the dry season); shaded verandahs in the bazaars; the Panna Meena Kund stepwell. It still works the same way today!

Singapore — a model of changing plans

A small island country with 8,000+ people per sq km, yet a good quality of life. The Concept Plan — land use up to 40 years ahead; the Master Plan — updated every 5 years. From a crowded port in the 1960s to a modern city. Mixed-use — homes, offices, parks and museums close together (Marina Bay Sands and Raffles Place are just a few km apart). The MRT metro, buses, cycle paths and green walkways → fewer cars. Vertical gardens on roofs and walls → a 'garden city', and less heat. Elders' homes are near hospitals, with childcare and meeting places — it is considered a 'blue zone' (a place where people live long, healthy lives, like Okinawa, Japan).

Good examples from India and the city of tomorrow

Sabarmati Riverfront (Ahmedabad) — a two-level riverside promenade, parks, markets, boats, and the Atal Bridge for walkers and cyclists. M.G. Road (Gangtok) — a pedestrian-only zone, very clean. What a good city needs: technology (smart traffic, online complaints, solar power), citizen participation, waste management, water, public transport, affordable housing, e-governance. Cities are 'alive' — they grow and change. The city of tomorrow is one that keeps a balance between development and nature, does not leave weaker people behind, and provides facilities without using up resources.

Theme C · Our Cultural Heritage and Knowledge Traditions

Cultural Currents: 13th to 17th Centuries

How culture flourished even amid unsettled politics — Bhakti and Sufi movements, music, miniature painting, gardens, women, education, literature, science, food and clothing.

Understand it in simple words the whole chapter, like a story

Read this section first, at your own pace — every idea comes with an example. Then revise from the short notes below.

What is culture, and why is India's culture so strong?

Culture = the shared way of life of a group that is passed down from generation to generation — values, customs, traditions, art, music, literature, philosophy, science. The ancient cultures of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece and Rome faded or disappeared with time, but Indian culture is alive even today — with deep roots, timeless values, a scientific outlook, and rich art and philosophy. Gandhiji said: 'I want the cultures of all lands to be blown about my house as freely as possible, but I refuse to be blown off my feet by any.' India adopted or reshaped in its own way the good things brought by people from outside (Greece's Megasthenes, Persians, Central Asians, Arab traders). From the 13th century new rulers brought new ideas; even so, three things continued: (1) traditions went on, (2) patronage of every kind of ruler, (3) participation of ordinary people.

Bhakti — reaching God without priests or rituals

There was a big difference between Islam, with one God and one book, and the Indian religions, with many schools of thought; several invaders broke temples and holy places. Yet society held together — one big reason was Bhakti. The Bhakti marga = a path to moksha (attaining truth / freedom from the cycle of birth and death) in which rituals and priests are not necessary; by the 6th century this had become an established tradition. In this period it became popular again — in local languages, with songs of love, compassion and equality, along with music and dance.

The saints and their messages

Kabirdas (15th century, UP; raised by a weaver couple) — one God; criticised the show and pretence of both Hindus and Muslims. His doha: 'when wheat is ground into flour, the difference between the grains disappears — in the same way Ram and Rahim become one'. Ravidas (early 16th century, from a family of leather workers) — equality; break down the walls of varna and caste. Guru Nanak — one God, equality of all; the Guru Granth Sahib even includes hymns of several Hindu saints! Mirabai (Rajasthan), Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (east), Tukaram, Dnyaneshwar, Namdev (west), Purandaradasa, Kanakadasa (south). In Tamil Nadu the Alvars' Divya Prabandham and the Nayanars' Tiruvachakam (hymns to Vishnu and Shiva of the 6th–9th centuries) continued to be sung. Pictures of saints were painted even 200 years later — their influence lasted that long!

Sufis — finding God through love

Sufism = the devotional path of Islam — an inner, personal search. Sufi saints sang qawwali — along with local classical music; Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, Nizamuddin Auliya. The whirling dervish (meditation in motion), zikr (repeating God's name again and again). Sufis also adopted the techniques of yoga and Vedanta — silence, renunciation, meditation. Rumi: 'Why should I seek? I am the same as He…' Bhakti and Sufi traditions often blended. Purandaradasa (15th–16th century) — the 'Father of Carnatic music'; he gave up the diamond trade to become a devotee of Krishna; ~4,75,000 kirtans! He reached the court of Krishnadevaraya; a postage stamp in 1964.

Music — two streams

The Natyashastra linked dance, drama, music, mathematics and beauty. Ways of telling stories: Rajasthan's kathputli (puppets), the pattachitra of Odisha, Bengal and Andhra (patta = cloth, chitra = picture) — stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Puranas. Classical music: Hindustani in the north, Carnatic in the south. In the late 15th century Raja Mansingh Tomar of Gwalior gave dhrupad (dhruva = steady, pada = verse) new heights. Tansen (Ramtanu Pandey) — one of Akbar's navratnas (nine jewels); influenced by a Sufi saint, yet he also composed works on Krishna and Shiva. Amir Khusro (13th–14th century) — an Indo-Persian Sufi poet; developed qawwali. Carnatic music developed almost separately from Persian-Arabic influence. The modern forms of the sitar and tabla date from this period (the tabla's roots lie in the older pakhawaj).

From wall to paper — miniature painting

Cave paintings → Ajanta → paper arrived in the 10th–11th century → small miniature paintings. Rajput painting (15th–19th century) — Hindu themes: lotus, banyan, elephants, cows, peacocks, women with large eyes. Abul Fazl wrote in the Ain-i-Akbari about Hindu painters: 'very few in the world are equal to them'. Mughal miniature — Persian influence; court scenes, landscapes, portraits; in Akbar's atelier (studio) one artist did the outline, another the colour, a third the finishing! Illustrations of the Razmnama (the Persian Mahabharata); Jahangir's favourite painter Abu al-Hasan. The two styles learned from each other; some Mughal painters moved to Bikaner.

Gardens — from udyana to charbagh

The Rigveda, Ramayana and Mahabharata mention udyana, vatika, nandana (gardens) — often with temples and ponds. The Mughals brought the Persian charbagh — four sections, with canals symbolising the rivers of paradise in the Quran, fountains and terraces. Nishat Bagh (Srinagar) — 17th century, with terraces and cascades. The garden at Amer Fort. The Mughal Gardens of Rashtrapati Bhavan (now Amrit Udyan).

The status of women — different from place to place

The 10th-century Arab historian Abu Zaidu-l Hasan: women were seen without veils in the courts of India's kings. After the 12th century, in the invasions women and children were enslaved and sold; some performed jauhar. For protection, purdah and ghunghat spread in parts of north India (less so in the south). Razia Sultan (13th century) — the first and only woman ruler of the Delhi Sultanate; she led her army herself, set up schools, and held court without a veil; her reign was short because of conspiracies. Nur Jahan (Jahangir's wife) — issued farmans, held court, and had coins in her name. In the south: the Portuguese Fernão Nunes saw women in Vijayanagara as wrestlers, astrologers, accountants and musicians; queens made gifts of entire villages to temples and built wells and rest-houses.

Education and knowledge — universities fell, but learning did not stop

Nalanda and Vikramashila were destroyed in the late 12th century — a great loss to humanity. Yet gurukuls, maths, temple schools, viharas, Jain pallis and village pathshalas continued; in the south Kanchipuram, Sringeri; and madrasas (Arabic, Persian, Hindavi). Apprenticeship in skills continued through jati and shreni (guilds). Vijayanagara — 'purvamaryade' (following the old religious customs). The Italian Pietro Della Valle (1623) saw children on the Karnataka coast learning reading, writing and arithmetic. Textiles: Koshti weavers; with Akbar's encouragement Gujarat's weavers went to Banaras; the Mughals brought zari/zardozi and brocade; the Banarasi saree has a GI tag. Pashmina shawls (cashmere from the goats of Ladakh and Kashmir) with the Persian buta (paisley) motif.

Literature, language and Dara Shikoh

Works were composed in Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Odia, Hindavi, Bengali and Marathi. Chaitanya's influence — the Bengali Dhamali folk songs; Kavindra Paramesvara made a Bengali translation of the Mahabharata. The Gajapati kings of Odisha were also patrons. Tirumalamba — a poet at Achyutadevaraya's court; the Sanskrit poem 'Varadambika Parinaya'. Persian was the language of administration; Faizi translated the Lilavati (Bhaskaracharya) into Persian, and the Rajatarangini too. Dara Shikoh — with the help of Varanasi's scholars, 52 Upanishads into Persian; these reached Europe and influenced 19th-century philosophers; Aurangzeb had him killed. Urdu — the Hindavi of the Ganga-Yamuna plains + Arabic-Persian words; in the Eighth Schedule; in the south, Dakhini. Amir Khusrau = the 'Father of Urdu literature'.

Science — calculus in Kerala!

Production of pure zinc at its peak — the first in the world, using distillation. 14th century — Narayana Pandita's Ganitakaumudi. In the south Madhava — the series expansion of trigonometric functions (the first in the world) and the infinite series for π — the 'Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics'. In 1500 Nilakantha Somayaji's model — Mercury and Venus orbit the Sun — came decades before Copernicus (1543)! In Akbar's time, saltpetre was used for gunpowder and for cooling water; Itr Jahangir (rose perfume). Medicine: Ayurveda, Siddha (Tamil Nadu), Unani (from West Asia).

Buildings, food and clothing

The Konark Sun Temple (13th century, Eastern Ganga) — 24 stone wheels. The Ranakpur Jain temple (15th century, Dhanna Seth; Rishabhanatha). The Sheesh Mahal, Amer (Raja Man Singh I) — a single lamp makes it sparkle like stars. The Ranganathaswamy temple at Srirangam — destroyed in the 14th century, rebuilt by the end of the same century. Food: cardamom, cloves, turmeric, coriander, black pepper; marinating in curd, ghee; khichdi in new flavours; under Jain-Hindu influence Akbar ate less meat; naan (Persian influence); samosa = Persian sambuseh (with potato and peas instead of meat and dried fruit!); jalebi = Persian zalabiyeh (sugar syrup instead of honey and rose water). Clothing: cotton, muslin and silk increased; in courts the jama and pyjama; the angrakha (from Persian fashion); the saree continued in its regional forms.

C. Short-answer (model answers)

  1. 1When can a renewable resource become non-renewable? Give two examples.

    When we use it faster than it can be replenished (restoration-regeneration) — for example, cutting forest timber so fast that trees cannot grow back, or over-hunting tuna fish. Punjab's groundwater has likewise become 'over-exploited'.

  2. 2How did Akbar's policies change over time?

    In his youth: harsh conquests like Chittor and mass killings; later: Rajput alliances, abolition of jizya, sulh-i-kul, discussions with scholars, a translation bureau (Razmnama) — political wisdom to stabilise the empire and make it acceptable to all.

  3. 3'The British took India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals' — explain.

    In the 18th century the Marathas were the biggest Indian power (controlling territory up to Delhi); they were the British's biggest enemy; only after the three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818) did the British become masters of all India.

  4. 4What is meant by 'Indians funded their own subjugation'?

    The cost of railways, telegraph, administration, the British army and wars was recovered from Indian taxes — although all this mainly served to strengthen British trade and rule.

  5. 5Why is universal adult franchise important?

    Every citizen aged 18+ gets one vote of equal value — no distinction of caste, religion, gender, education or income; everyone has a say in deciding the country's future; representatives are accountable to the people.

  6. 6What is the difference between the Legislature and the Executive?

    The Legislature (Parliament: President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha) makes laws and keeps a watch on the executive; the Executive (President, VP, the PM's Council of Ministers) implements laws, brings most bills, and is accountable to the Lok Sabha.

  7. 7What is the demographic dividend, and what should India do?

    The economic benefit that comes from having a large young working-age population (India ~65% aged 15–59). The window lasts until 2050; so India needs good education, skills, health, jobs and facilities like daycare for women.

  8. 8How did Bhakti and Sufi traditions bring society together?

    Both spread the message of love, one God and equality through local languages, music and dance; they weakened rituals and caste barriers; the Sufis learnt from yoga-Vedanta; saints like Kabir sang of the unity of Ram and Rahim.
